\begin{abstract}
\noindent Greenwashing moves to where enforcement is weakest. We show this in pre-registered experiments in an agent-based market initialised on CSI~300 firms, where 1,000 investors price reported ESG scores and 200 firms choose between costly real improvement and cheap inflation that audits may detect. China's statutory fine leaves large firms inflating in two thirds of their decisions, and heavier fines add little, because inflation added to an undetected case is not fined again. When firms know the audit rule, auditing by market value moves inflation to small firms and auditing score jumps keeps it below the threshold; both do worse than random audits with the same budget. Letting firms choose their assurance provider moves inflation to the laxest one. At the doses tested, raising transparency improves true ESG more than quadrupling the fine, but the advantage falls from 0.2 to 0.003--0.012 standard deviations when true performance reverts to the mean.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} greenwashing; ESG disclosure; agent-based model; inspection game; impact investing; audit targeting; third-party assurance; pre-registration.\\
\noindent\textbf{JEL:} C63, G18, G23, K22, K42, M14, Q56.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

This paper shows that greenwashing moves to where enforcement is weakest, and that this is largely why making inflated ESG reports easier to see through raises firms' true ESG performance more than raising fines does. We establish both in pre-registered experiments in an artificial stock market where 1,000 investors trade the shares of 200 firms initialised on CSI~300 constituents. Every period each firm chooses between costly real improvement, cheap inflation of its report that audits may detect, and doing nothing; impact investors pay for reported scores; and a regulator sets transparency, audits and fines. Neither instrument has been applied to ESG reports at scale and true performance is not observed, so we compare them in otherwise identical simulated markets.

The question is open in China. Exchange guidelines require several hundred large listed companies to publish sustainability reports from the 2025 reporting year but provide only disciplinary sanctions \citep[e.g.][]{sse2024guideline}, and it is not settled whether the Securities Law's fine for false disclosure, capped at CNY~10 million per case \citep[Art.~197]{securitieslaw2019}, reaches ESG reports at all. That cap is about 0.01\% of the median market value of a CSI~300 constituent. In the model, fixed fines of this kind miss in two places. Under the statutory fine, large firms inflate in 66\% of their decisions and small firms in 6\%. And moving from no fine to the statutory fine cuts the share of inflating decisions from 91\% to 22\%, but eight times that fine still leaves 12.5\%, because a firm with an undetected case adds new inflation to it and faces no new fine. When every false report is fined separately, this plateau disappears.

Redirecting enforcement moves these gaps rather than closing them. At an unchanged expected number of audits, auditing in proportion to market value moves greenwashing to the more numerous small firms, whose audit rate falls: counted by decisions, small firms make 57\% of all inflating decisions, against 14\% under random audits. Auditing firms whose reported scores jump moves it below the jump threshold: when the rule is public, 44\% of new inflations are flagged at the next audit date, against 54\% when it is hidden. Letting firms choose their assurance provider moves it to the laxest one: 96.9\% of assured firms' inflating decisions are made by clients of the year's lowest-quality provider. Each shift also appears without natural decay of the true level; what decay changes is its cost in true ESG. In the main setting, value targeting lowers the equal-weighted true level by 0.19 standard deviations (SD) relative to random audits and jump targeting by 0.14~SD, although both find inflation more often; with the rule hidden, neither differs significantly from random audits, so the loss appears to come mainly from firms' response to a known rule. With strong liability and providers assigned at random, mandatory assurance raises the true level by 0.27~SD, and firm choice loses most of that gain ($-0.20$~SD against assignment). Without natural decay the same four contrasts are $-0.003$, $-0.008$, $+0.030$ and $-0.010$~SD (Sections~\ref{sec:targeted} and~\ref{sec:assurance}).

Transparency is harder to route around, because it lowers the payoff of every inflated report, whatever the firm's size, its history or who inspects it. Raising transparency from 0.1 to 0.6 raises the true level more than quadrupling the fixed fine, and both beat the baseline. With a common washing cost and lasting improvement, inflation stops altogether above a transparency of about 0.57, the threshold that an inspection-game benchmark of the firm's decision predicts (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}; \citealp{avenhaus2002inspection}). The doses are not matched on expected penalty or implementation cost, so this is a ranking at the doses tested, not a comparison at equal cost.

The advantage of transparency depends on real improvement lasting. The effect of transparency against the baseline is $+0.04$~SD without natural decay of the true level and $+0.33$~SD at 0.2~SD per year, and its advantage over the quadrupled fine is $+0.205$~SD at 0.2. When true levels instead revert to the cross-sectional mean, at two rates suggested by an unverified rating panel and used as sensitivity values, improvements fade while inflation persists, the transparency threshold rises above 0.6, and the advantage falls to $+0.003$ to $+0.006$~SD. A pre-registered test on new seeds that adds a fine for every false report keeps the ordering in all four settings, but the advantage stays at $+0.004$ to $+0.012$~SD. In our experiments the ordering of the two instruments is robust; the size of the advantage is not.

Two further results bound these conclusions. With the financing contract of the main experiments, every policy works only through investor preference: without impact investors' taste for reported ESG, firms neither improve nor inflate and no policy has any effect, whereas with it the true level rises by 0.79~SD over fourteen years. This is because a firm's financing rate then responds to its rating only through its share price. If lenders priced the rating directly, firms would have a financing incentive even without investor taste; we have not run that case (Appendix~\ref{app:financing}). Whether real investors respond to ratings is an empirical question, and a pre-registered test on Huazheng rating updates from 2023 to 2025 gives a partial answer (Appendix~\ref{app:investors}): an upgrade goes with higher fund ownership at year-end, and a one-SD higher score with an implied cost of equity 14.5 basis points lower [3.0, 26.0], about half the 28 basis points the calibration targets; returns in the two months after an update show no statistically significant response. And the market's response to policy is modest in the main setting. Replaying firms' decisions on the baseline run's prices and investor population shows that market feedback adds about 11\% to the fixed-fine effect, and its net effect on the transparency policy cannot be distinguished from zero.

Design, seeds, metrics and primary questions were committed to a public repository before each confirmatory run: 59 scenarios with 30 paired seeds in the first experiment, 1,770 simulated markets of 3,500 trading days, and new seeds for the experiments on targeted audits and assurance providers and for the joint robustness test. Supplements designed after the first results are labelled exploratory, and each was written down before it was run. Every run is kept in the archive, failed ones included.

The results inherit the model's limits. Firms are myopic net-present-value maximisers. A tabular Q-learner trained on realised accounts failed a pre-set coverage test, and in a pre-registered redo it inflated under the information policy in 23.7\% of decisions against 3.8\% for an NPV firm, at a median loss of 0.042 basis points per decision, too small to learn from (Section~\ref{sec:learning}); the main results say nothing about boundedly rational firms. The costs of transparency and audits are not modelled. And with the calibrated investor taste, a firm's financing rate responds to the signal investors see by about 2.2 basis points per SD, an order of magnitude below what we estimate in our own sample: a one-SD higher Huazheng score goes with a debt cost 28.0 basis points lower in the following year, an association rather than a causal effect, and 11.9 basis points within firms. A pre-registered attempt to close the gap failed: when lenders price the rating directly at the estimated size, most firms push their ratings close to the top of the scale and ratings lose the persistence they have in the data (Appendix~\ref{app:financing}). Our magnitudes therefore hold for the smaller financing incentive.

The paper relates to a large literature showing that misconduct moves to where enforcement is weakest. A penalty that does not rise with the size of an offence gives no reason to keep the offence small \citep{stigler1970optimum,mookherjee1994marginal}. Air quality is worse on days that a known monitoring schedule leaves unrecorded \citep{zou2021unwatched}; in an Indian field experiment, auditors chosen and paid by the plants they audited reported emissions just below the standard \citep{duflo2013truth}; and emissions and polluting plants move to where regulatory or environmental pressure is weaker \citep{bartram2022real,duchin2025sustainability}. These studies measure one setting at a time, where outcomes can be observed. Our first contribution is to bring these margins together for sustainability reports, whose enforcement is still being settled and whose underlying performance is not observed, and to compare in one market instruments that empirical work cannot compare in a single setting: fixed fines booked per case or per report, known and hidden audit rules, assigned and chosen assurance providers, and transparency. Second, theories of greenwashing treat it as selective disclosure that an activist may audit \citep{lyon2011greenwash}, and in the closest agent-based work greenwashing arises passively from pooled disclosure categories, without audits or fines \citep{zhao2025agent,zhao2026green}. Our firms choose how much to misreport under audits and fines, differ in size and finance themselves in a market whose prices and investor population respond to policy, and the premium that investors' taste creates in equilibrium models of sustainable investing \citep{heinkel2001effect,pastor2021sustainable,pedersen2021responsible} rewards real improvement and greenwashing alike. Third, we bring pre-analysis plans \citep{olken2015promises} to agent-based policy analysis. Section~\ref{sec:literature} discusses related work in more detail.

\section{Related literature}\label{sec:literature}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Greenwashing and ESG disclosure.} \citet{lyon2011greenwash} model greenwash as selective disclosure by a firm that an activist may audit, and \citet{delmas2011drivers} and \citet{lyon2015means} review its drivers and forms. Empirically, firms both exaggerate and understate their sustainability performance \citep{kim2015greenwash}, and selective disclosure is less common where firms face more scrutiny \citep{marquis2016scrutiny}. On disclosure mandates, \citet{christensen2021mandatory} review the economics of mandatory sustainability reporting; \citet{chen2018effect} find that China's 2008 mandate lowered the profitability of affected firms and reduced pollution in the cities most exposed to them; \citet{fiechter2022real} find that the EU directive raised affected firms' CSR activity. ESG ratings of the same firm diverge widely across providers \citep{berg2022aggregate}, one reason we treat the reported score as a proxy rather than the true attribute. Our information instrument is a stylised version of these mechanisms; unlike reduced-form studies, we can switch it on in otherwise identical markets and compare it with fines.

\paragraph{Enforcement.} The deterrence logic of fines goes back to \citet{becker1968crime}. \citet{stigler1970optimum} pointed out that a penalty that does not rise with the size of an offence gives no reason to keep the offence small, and \citet{mookherjee1994marginal} characterise optimal enforcement when offenders choose among levels of an activity. A fixed fine booked per case is an instance: once a case is open, adding to it enlarges the false report but not the fine (Section~\ref{sec:instruments}). Inspection games formalise the interaction of an inspector who audits with some probability and an inspectee who may violate \citep{avenhaus2002inspection}; we use the inspectee's side as an analytical benchmark for the firm layer. The audit probability in our model is an assumption; as background, \citet{dyck2024pervasive} estimate that only about a third of corporate fraud is detected.

\paragraph{Where misconduct goes.} Regulated parties respond to how enforcement is allocated, not only to how strict it is. Under a once-every-six-day monitoring schedule in the United States, air quality is worse on unmonitored days \citep{zou2021unwatched}. In China, after water quality readings were linked to officials' promotion, regulation became tighter for firms just upstream of a monitoring station than for those just downstream \citep{he2020watering}, and automating air quality monitoring raised reported PM\textsubscript{10} concentrations by 35\% \citep{greenstone2022technology}. Financially constrained firms shifted emissions and output from California to other states after its cap-and-trade programme \citep{bartram2022real}, and firms under environmental pressure sell polluting plants to buyers under less pressure, without a fall in pollution \citep{duchin2025sustainability}. Where firms choose and pay their auditors, auditors in an Indian field experiment reported emissions just below the standard, and a reform of that audit market made reports more truthful and lowered pollution \citep{duflo2013truth}; in models of credit ratings, issuers who can shop among agencies disclose inflated ratings \citep{skreta2009ratings,bolton2012credit}. Information can close margins that penalties leave open. In Denmark, evasion is close to zero for income that third parties report, and audit threats change only self-reported income \citep{kleven2011unwilling}; in Chile, announced monitoring changes transactions that leave a paper trail less than others \citep{pomeranz2015taxation}. Our targeted audits, assurance providers and transparency are stylised counterparts of these margins, brought together in one market for sustainability reports, where true performance is not observed.

\paragraph{Sustainable investing and the cost of capital.} When some investors exclude polluting firms, those firms' cost of capital rises and they may reform \citep{heinkel2001effect}; in equilibrium, investors' taste for green holdings lowers the expected returns of green assets \citep{pastor2021sustainable,pedersen2021responsible}. Empirical estimates of the financing effect range from 7--18 basis points on US bank loans \citep{goss2011impact} to about 0.19 percentage points of the cost of equity per rating notch for Chinese listed firms \citep{zhao2024esg}, which we use as the calibration target. In our model the premium is both the reward for real improvement and the reward for greenwashing, and the share of investors who pay it evolves.

\paragraph{Agent-based models of green disclosure.} The closest work is by \citet{zhao2025agent} and \citet{zhao2026green}. They simulate firms at three green levels that compete for a fixed pool of ESG fund capital allocated by investors' valuation of each level, and they use empirical game-theoretic analysis to find equilibrium upgrade strategies under strict and lax disclosure and under a two-stage policy that switches from lax to strict. The journal version extends it to a dual-market model of the EU and China. In their model a firm decides only whether to upgrade. Greenwashing is passive: lax disclosure pools low- and high-green firms at their average valuation, and no firm chooses to misreport. There are no audits or fines, and firms differ by green level rather than size. Our paper differs in four respects. Firms actively choose how much to inflate a report, at a cost and under a risk of detection. Enforcement has an explicit audit-and-penalty structure, in which fines are booked per case, fixed and proportional fines are compared, and the statutory cap is calibrated. Firm size matters because a lump-sum fine shrinks relative to value as firms grow, which produces the size divide of Section~\ref{sec:size}. And financing gains come from a market whose prices and investor population respond to policy, so we can measure how much that feedback contributes (Section~\ref{sec:feedback}). Their method answers a question our simulation does not: it builds an empirical payoff table from simulations and applies $\alpha$-Rank, which ranks strategy profiles by their weight in the stationary distribution of an evolutionary dynamic, so it says which profiles a population of firms would persist in.

\paragraph{Agent-based finance and research practice.} Artificial stock markets with heterogeneous, adaptive agents go back to \citet{arthur1997asset}; \citet{lebaron2006agent} surveys the field and \citet{farmer2009economy} argue for its use in policy analysis. We parameterise demand elasticities in the range of the demand-system literature \citep{koijen2019demand} and describe the model following the logic of the ODD protocol \citep{grimm2020odd}. Agent-based studies leave many choices to the researcher; following the case for pre-analysis plans in economics \citep{olken2015promises}, we fixed design, seeds and inference before the confirmatory run and keep failed runs in the record.

\section{The model}\label{sec:model}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Overview and timing}

A simulation runs for 3,500 trading days (252 per year, about 14 years). Day~0 initialises the market; days 1--1200 are a pre-policy period under the baseline regime; a policy, if any, switches on at day 1201 and stays in place. Outcomes are measured over the policy window, days 1200--3500. The agents are 1,000 investors of two types, 200 firms in three size tiers (the largest 10\%, the next 30\% and the remaining 60\% by initial value), market makers with an aggregate noise-trader account, and a regulator. Figure~\ref{fig:schematic} shows how they interact. Table~\ref{tab:parameters} lists the main parameters with their sources.

\subsection{Firms and the disclosure state}

Each firm $i$ has a latent true level $g_i$, measured in units of its initial cross-sectional standard deviation (SD), and undetected inflation $x_i \ge 0$; its report is $g_i + x_i$. Investors observe the signal
\begin{equation}
s_i = \min\{\, g_i + (1-\tau)\,x_i,\; C \,\},
\label{eq:signal}
\end{equation}
where $\tau \in [0,1]$ is transparency, the share of inflation that investors see through, and $C$ is the latent value of a perfect score of 100 points. Reports cannot exceed a perfect score, so inflation is capped at $\max\{C - g_i, 0\}$. Without effort, the true level decays by $\delta$~SD per year (main setting $\delta = 0.2$).

Every 60 trading days each firm takes one of three actions. \emph{Improve} by $\Delta$ costs $c_i\Delta^2$ and raises $g_i$ after an implementation delay of 120 days. \emph{Inflate} by $\Delta$ costs $w\,c_i\Delta^2$ and enters the published report after 20 days. \emph{Maintain} costs nothing. Changes are capped at 0.25~SD per period. Improvement beyond the report's remaining room replaces inflation, so only its transparent share is newly visible. The marginal cost scale is $c_i = \bar c\, m_{k(i)}\, \varepsilon_i\, e^{\eta g_i}$, with $\bar c = 0.05$, size multipliers $m = (0.6, 1, 1.6)$ for large, mid and small firms, lognormal dispersion $\varepsilon_i$ with log-SD 0.5, and $\eta = 1.5$, so improvement becomes dearer as the level rises. The size multipliers assume that large firms improve more cheaply; an equal-cost control removes them. The washing cost ratio $w = 0.2$ has no empirical basis and is varied in the experiment. Costs, fines and subsidies change a firm's intrinsic value and reach its price through the market makers on the next day.

\subsection{Investors and prices}

Investors differ in wealth (lognormal), risk aversion $\gamma_j$ and decision calendar (median 20 days). On a decision day an investor sets its equity share to the Merton ratio $(E[R_m]-r_f)/(\gamma_j \sigma_m^2)$, clipped to $[0,1]$, and allocates equity across firms by a softmax tilt away from market weights,
\begin{equation}
w_{ji} \propto P_i \exp(\kappa_j U_{ji}), \qquad U_{ji} = E[R_i] - \gamma_j\,\mathrm{cov}(r_i, r_m) + \theta_j\, s_i ,
\label{eq:utility}
\end{equation}
moving half-way from its current to its target holdings. Profit-maximising investors (PM) have $\theta_j = 0$. Impact investors (II) have a lognormal taste $\theta_j$, measured as an annual-return equivalent per SD of the signal, with median $\bar\theta$ and log-SD 0.5. With equal utilities every investor holds the market portfolio, which keeps the rule consistent with a fixed supply of shares.

Each firm's shares are quoted by a market maker who passes through news about intrinsic value and moves the price with order flow. Price impact has a permanent and a transient part, depth thins when volatility rises, and an aggregate noise-trader account adds correlated flow. Seven market parameters were fitted by the method of simulated moments to return moments of US industry portfolios; cash and shares are conserved every day. The market layer was frozen before any policy experiment, and its known defects are reported in Section~\ref{sec:validity}.

\subsection{Investor evolution}

Every 60 days, 10\% of investors review their type. Each reviewer samples one other investor uniformly; if the partner is of the other type, the reviewer switches with probability $k \cdot \max\{0, \bar G_{\text{other}} - \bar G_{\text{own}}\}$, where $\bar G$ is the mean wealth growth of each type over the window and $k = 100$. This is the proportional imitation rule of \citet{schlag1998imitate}. The expected change in the impact-investor share $s$ is $m\,k\,s(1-s)\,E[\bar G_{II} - \bar G_{PM}]$ with review share $m$, a discrete replicator dynamic \citep{taylor1978evolutionary}, so the share does not drift when the two types perform equally. An earlier implementation omitted the partner draw and drifted towards one half even at zero taste. With the corrected rule, the change in the share over 14 years at zero taste has 95\% intervals that include zero at initial shares of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 (20 seeds each), and a taste near the calibrated value raises it by 2.3 percentage points [1.4, 3.1] from an initial share of 0.2: the market's own drive towards impact investing is small.

\subsection{Financing gains and the firm's decision rule}

Each firm has a fixed external financing exposure $E_i$, half of its initial market value, whose fee rate $r_f + 0.05\, E_i/(E_i + P_i^{\text{lag}})$ is repriced every 60 days. A firm estimates the local permanent response of its price to its own signal, holding everything else fixed, and converts it into $B_i$, the annual financing saving per SD of signal as a share of current market value. Discounting at 5\% a year, the calendar of delays, repricing dates and audits gives three factors. The signal annuities of real improvement, $A_r$, and of inflation, $A_w$ (which lasts only until an audit), count financing savings over a five-year horizon. The expected discounted value $D$ of paying one unit when new inflation is detected has no horizon: a false report remains a liability until an audit finds it, so $D$ sums the audit probability over all future audit dates. With subsidy $\sigma$ per SD, proportional fine $p$ per SD of detected inflation, fixed fine $F$ and market value $P$, the values of the three actions are
\begin{align}
V_I &= \frac{(B A_r + \sigma)^2}{4c}, \label{eq:vi}\\
V_W &= \frac{\max\{B(1-\tau)A_w - pD,\, 0\}^2}{4wc} - \pi \frac{F}{P} D, \label{eq:vw}\\
V_M &= 0, \nonumber
\end{align}
with optimal sizes $(BA_r+\sigma)/2c$ and $(B(1-\tau)A_w - pD)/2wc$ before caps. The term $\pi$ is the probability that new inflation opens a new case, which is zero for a firm that already carries undetected inflation (see below). The firm takes the action with the highest value and maintains on ties. The rule is myopic: firms re-optimise every period and do not anticipate their own future choices.

\subsection{The regulator}

In each 60-day period every firm is audited with probability 1\%. An audit that finds inflation opens a case: the firm pays the fixed fine $F$ plus $p$ times its inflation, and the inflation is removed. Inflation added while earlier inflation is still undetected joins the same case and adds no fixed fine. We call this \emph{per-case booking}; the alternative, a separate fine for every false report, is tested in an exploratory supplement (Appendix~\ref{app:perreport}). In the main setting $p = 0$ and $F$ equals CNY~10 million, the statutory maximum, which is $4.56\times10^{-5}$ of the average firm's initial value. Baseline transparency is $\tau = 0.1$. The second experiment (Section~\ref{sec:targeted}) replaces random audits with targeted rules.

\subsection{Initialisation and calibration}

The market is initialised on the CSI~300 constituents of 9 October 2026, matched 300 of 300 on Huazheng composite ESG scores, half-year book equity and market value (Table~\ref{tab:parameters}). Scores are mapped to the latent standard-normal level by their empirical quantiles, so the scale saturates at a perfect score, and the latent level is linked to log value by a Gaussian copula with $\rho = 0.177$, converted from the sample Spearman correlation of 0.169. The dispersion of log intrinsic value, 0.915, is the cross-sectional SD of log market value.

One parameter was calibrated to a target. We set the median taste $\bar\theta = 0.0724$ so that, before any policy, the cross-sectional slope of dividend yield on the signal is about $-28$ basis points per SD (validation median $-28.25$). The target combines the estimate of \citet{zhao2024esg}, 0.19 percentage points of the cost of equity per Huazheng rating notch, with the 1.45 notches per SD in our sample; it is a cross-sectional association, not a causal effect. In our own sample, with Huazheng scores of October 2022--2024 and the implied cost of equity of \citet{easton2004pe} eight months later, the association is 14.5 basis points per SD [3.0, 26.0] (Appendix~\ref{app:investors}), so the target lies just above that interval. We kept the calibration, which was fixed before the experiments. The audit probability of 1\% per period is an assumption. It implies a discounted value of about 0.455 for paying one unit on detection; this is a present-value factor, not a detection rate, and is not comparable with the estimate of \citet{dyck2024pervasive} that about a third of corporate fraud is detected, which we cite only as evidence that detection is far from certain. The remaining firm-layer parameters --- natural decay, the washing cost ratio, the cost function, delays and the financing contract --- are also assumptions, and the experiment varies the ones that matter most.

\section{Analytical benchmark}\label{sec:benchmark}
%======================================================================

The firm's decision rule is the inspectee's best response in an inspection game with given audit probability, fines and transparency. Two propositions summarise it; both describe the rule in equations \eqref{eq:vi}--\eqref{eq:vw} for an interior solution.

\begin{proposition}[Inflating under a fixed fine]\label{prop:fixed}
Let $\sigma = 0$, $p = 0$, $\pi = 1$ and $\tau < \tau^*$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold}). A firm inflates if and only if
\[
B^2\Big[\frac{(1-\tau)^2 A_w^2}{w} - A_r^2\Big] > 4\, c\, \frac{F}{P}\, D,
\]
that is, if and only if its market value exceeds $P^* = 4cFD \big/ \big(B^2[(1-\tau)^2A_w^2/w - A_r^2]\big)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proofsketch}
With $B>0$, $V_I>0$, so inflating must beat improving: $B^2(1-\tau)^2A_w^2/4wc - FD/P > B^2A_r^2/4c$.
\end{proofsketch}

Three consequences follow. The fixed fine decides \emph{whether} a firm inflates, not \emph{how much}, unlike a proportional fine, which lowers the net gain per SD. Because $F/P$ falls with size while $B$ is roughly size-independent and large firms improve more cheaply, large firms cross $P^*$ first; under a proportional fine the condition becomes $B[(1-\tau)A_w - \sqrt{w}A_r] > pD$, in which size enters only through $B$. And for a firm with an open, undetected case ($\pi = 0$) the condition reduces to $\tau < \tau^*$ and no longer depends on $F$: a higher fine can stop firms from starting to inflate but not from continuing. At an audit probability of 1\% per period, an undetected case lasts about 100 periods on average.

\begin{proposition}[Transparency threshold]\label{prop:threshold}
Let $\sigma = 0$ and $p = 0$. Firm $i$ inflates if and only if $\tau < \tau_i^*$, where
\[
\tau_i^* = 1 - \frac{\sqrt{w}}{A_w}\sqrt{A_r^2 + \frac{4\, c_i\, \pi_i\, (F/P_i)\, D}{B_i^2}}\,,
\]
and $\tau_i^* \le \tau^* \equiv 1 - \sqrt{w}\,A_r/A_w$, with equality if and only if $\pi_i F = 0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proofsketch}
Solve the inequality of Proposition~\ref{prop:fixed} for $1-\tau$.
\end{proofsketch}

Without a fine, every firm has the same threshold $\tau^*$, which depends only on $w$ and the timing that determines $A_w/A_r$, not on costs, size or financing gains. With the default timing $A_w/A_r \approx 1.03$, so $\tau^*(0.2) \approx 0.57$. A fixed fine spreads the thresholds below $\tau^*$, and firms that already inflate keep the threshold $\tau^*$.

The propositions hold the financing gain $B$ fixed and ignore other firms. In the simulation, $B_i$ and $P_i$ move with prices and with the investor population, firms carry different states and open cases, and policies change all of these. The experiment therefore asks two things the propositions cannot answer: how large the policy effects are once financing gains come from a market, and how much the feedback from policy to $B$ and $P$ changes them. The threshold prediction itself is close to a property of the decision rule: above $\tau^*(w)$ no firm can find inflation worthwhile whatever $B$ is. Its confirmation in the simulation (Section~\ref{sec:threshold}) is therefore a check of the implementation under market feedback rather than independent evidence.

\paragraph{Outcomes and questions.} The primary metric is the change in the equal-weighted mean true level from day 1200 to day 3500, in initial SD. Four primary questions were tested two-sided with the \citet{holm1979simple} correction:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item[P1] market preference: B minus market-off B, over the full period (days 0--3500);
\item[P2] instrument comparison: I minus F;
\item[P3] interaction: (IF $-$ B) $-$ (market-off IF $-$ market-off B);
\item[P4] size mechanism: B minus equal-cost B, in the large-minus-small difference of the change.
\end{itemize}
The other 36 contrasts are reported with unadjusted intervals and make no confirmatory claim. Because all scenarios in a market setting share their history up to the switch, contrasts are paired by seed, and intervals are paired 95\% $t$ intervals over the 30 seeds.

\paragraph{Phase classification.} A world is classified by its terminal-year change in the true level net of the decay it would have had without action ($G$) and its terminal-year inflation ($W$), with threshold 0.25~SD: real improvement ($G \ge 0.25$, $W < 0.25$), greenwashing trap ($G < 0.25$, $W \ge 0.25$), low improvement and low inflation, or mixed. A cell is labelled with a state only if at least 80\% of its seeds share it. All nine combinations of thresholds in $\{0.125, 0.25, 0.5\}$ are in the archive.

\paragraph{Failures.} Every run is kept. Of 1,770 runs, all completed and 37 triggered the numerical price guard, a mechanical validation gate; they remain in the statistics, and excluding them leaves P2 at $+0.207$ and P3 at $+0.333$.

\paragraph{Exploratory supplements.} After the confirmatory results we ran five supplements, each written into the contract and committed before its own implementation and runs: three intermediate decay rates (360 runs); a counterfactual that removes market feedback (360 reruns and replays); the same decomposition in stronger markets (270 runs); a split of the feedback into two channels (240 runs); and three threats to the main claims, namely mean-reverting true levels, a fine for every false report and firm-specific washing costs (600 runs). New options default to the original behaviour, and rerunning stage-F scenarios with them off reproduces the archive bit for bit. They use the same seeds and carry no confirmatory weight.

\subsection{How to read magnitudes: natural decay}\label{sec:decay}

Natural decay matters because it makes room for inflation: a firm that does nothing sees its score slide and can restore it cheaply by inflating. Figure~\ref{fig:decay} shows the two policy effects at the five tested decay rates. Without decay, only 4.7\% of baseline decisions inflate and the effect of the information policy is $+0.040$~SD; at 0.2~SD per year, 22.3\% inflate and the effect is $+0.331$~SD. The fine's effect grows from $+0.005$ to $+0.125$~SD and the gap between the two instruments from $+0.035$ to $+0.205$~SD. Every interval excludes zero, and the ordering I $>$ F $>$ B holds at all five rates: at the two pre-registered end points and at the three intermediate rates of the exploratory supplement. The growth is convex: at 0.1~SD per year the information effect is 39\% of its value at 0.2. Unless decay is close to 0.2, effects are closer to the lower end of the range.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_decay.pdf}
\caption{Policy effects on the true level over the policy window (days 1200--3500) at five uniform rates of natural decay. Points are means over 30 paired seeds; bars are paired 95\% $t$ intervals. Filled points are confirmatory; hollow points come from the exploratory decay supplement.}
\label{fig:decay}
\end{figure}

We therefore report magnitudes as the range from no decay to 0.2~SD per year. Results available only for the main setting --- doses, proportional fines, size and the phase diagrams --- hold under the assumption of 0.2~SD per year and are cited with that condition. The three main contrasts grow with decay, but we have not tested how these other results vary with it, so we do not claim they are upper bounds. Absolute levels are not interpretable: under the assumed decay every scenario declines (B by $-1.196$~SD), and with market preference switched off the decline, $-1.825$~SD, is exactly the decay accumulated over 2,300 days. Only differences between scenarios carry information.

Uniform decay may also be the wrong form. In an unverified panel of Chinese ESG ratings, ratings show no trend and revert towards the middle. When true levels instead revert to the cross-sectional mean at two data-motivated sensitivity rates, the ordering I $>$ F $>$ B survives, but the advantage of information over the quadrupled fine falls to $+0.003$ to $+0.006$~SD: reversion erodes real improvement while inflation is removed only by audits, and transparency of 0.6 no longer stops inflation (Appendix~\ref{app:reversion}).

\subsection{Investor preference is necessary}\label{sec:preference}

Impact investors' taste raises the true level over the full period by $+0.795$~SD [$+0.743$, $+0.847$] (P1, Holm $p = 3\times10^{-23}$; Table~\ref{tab:primary}). With taste switched off, firms take no action under any policy: improvement earns nothing and inflation is not needed to hold the signal, so the four policies produce identical worlds. The interaction P3, $+0.331$ [$+0.295$, $+0.366$], therefore equals the effect of IF with the market on. It is mechanical rather than a finding about how two forces combine: in this model, policy acts only through the preference of investors.

\subsection{The transparency threshold}\label{sec:threshold}

Figure~\ref{fig:threshold} shows the share of inflating decisions on the grid of washing cost ratios and transparency levels, with the threshold $\tau^*(w)$ predicted by Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold}. In all 16 cells the prediction holds: no decision inflates once $\tau \ge \tau^*(w)$, and some do below it. Below the threshold the share declines gradually with transparency, because fines spread firm thresholds. Inflation already in reports does not vanish at the switch: at $w = 0.2$, terminal-year inflation is 1.33~SD at the baseline transparency of 0.1 and 0.35~SD at 0.6, not zero, since only audits, at 1\% per period, or real improvement remove old inflation. Phase diagram 2 has a region of real improvement only where washing is expensive ($w = 0.4$) and transparency is at least 0.6: in each of these two cells, 27 of the 29 classified seeds are real improvement (one seed per cell failed core validation and is not classified).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{figures/fig_threshold.pdf}
\caption{Share of firm decisions in the policy window that inflate the report, by washing cost ratio $w$ and transparency $\tau$ (means over 30 seeds). The black line marks the threshold $\tau^*(w)$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold}; the prediction is that no firm inflates to the right of it. Main setting.}
\label{fig:threshold}

Three qualifications matter for how this result is used. First, as Section~\ref{sec:benchmark} explains, the upper bound of the threshold depends only on $w$ and timing, which the market cannot move; the 16 of 16 match confirms that the simulation implements the rule under feedback, not that real firms behave this way. Second, the threshold is sharp because every firm has the same washing cost ratio. If the ratio differs across firms, the aggregate response is governed by those that inflate most cheaply: in the cells with $w = 0.05$, 19\% of decisions still inflate at $\tau = 0.6$, and inflation stops only near 0.78. Third, the threshold assumes that real improvement lasts. Under mean reversion it rises to about 0.8 (Appendix~\ref{app:reversion}). With firm-specific washing costs, inflation declines firm by firm instead of stopping at a common threshold, and the policy comparison barely moves (Appendix~\ref{app:hetero}).

\subsection{Fine design and the size divide}\label{sec:size}

The size statistic in this section is the large-minus-small difference in the \emph{change} of the true level over the policy window, days 1200--3500; it says which firms improve more during the window, not how firms rank at its end. Under the baseline, the true level of large firms changes by 0.33~SD less than that of small firms, because large firms inflate in 66\% of their decisions and small firms in 6\%. The fine is the reason: CNY~10 million is a negligible share of a large firm's value, so large firms cross the value threshold $P^*$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:fixed} first. Counted by decisions, large firms, a tenth of all firms, make 31.7\% of all inflating decisions in the rerun of B in the targeted-audit experiment (Section~\ref{sec:targeted}), and 65.1\% without natural decay. The cost advantage assumed for large firms works the other way. Removing it widens the difference from $-0.33$ to $-0.56$~SD (P4: $+0.229$ [$+0.205$, $+0.252$]), so the difference comes from inflation, not from costs. Under the information policy the sign changes: large firms' true level changes by 0.38~SD \emph{more} than small firms' over the window.

Raising the fixed fine eightfold lowers inflation in every tier but still leaves large firms inflating in 48\% of their decisions (66\% at the statutory fine). A proportional fine reverses the size pattern, as Proposition~\ref{prop:fixed} predicts: large firms now inflate least (72\% against 88\% for small firms at $1\times$). But at the equal-amount reference it is much weaker overall. It leaves 84\% of decisions inflating and lowers the true change relative to the fixed fine of the same multiple by $-0.338$~SD [$-0.359$, $-0.318$] at $1\times$, $-0.358$ at $2\times$ and $-0.290$ at $4\times$. The reason is the reference point: per-period inflation is far below 1~SD, so a fine of CNY~10 million per SD of inflation is much lighter per decision than a lump sum of the same amount. The result shows how the two designs differ at this reference; it does not show that either is stronger in general.

\subsection{What the market adds}\label{sec:feedback}

A firm's decision depends on other firms and on investors only through its financing gain $B_i$ and its market value $P_i$, and audit draws come from a separate random stream shared across scenarios. Given the paths of $B$ and $P$ and the audit draws, the firm layer can therefore be replayed without the market. Replaying each run on its own paths reproduces it bit for bit (360 of 360). Replaying a policy scenario on the \emph{baseline} run's paths gives the policy effect without feedback, and the difference from the full model is the contribution of feedback from policy to prices, financing gains and the investor population.

In the main setting this feedback is small and its sign differs by contrast (Table~\ref{tab:feedback}). Its net effect on the information policy cannot be distinguished from zero ($-0.008$~SD [$-0.022$, $+0.007$]). It strengthens the fixed-fine effect by 11\% of that effect ($+0.014$~SD) and narrows the gap between the instruments by 10\% ($-0.021$~SD). The equal-cost and no-decay settings give shares of the same order (10--13\% where identifiable). Where investor taste is doubled, feedback strengthens the fixed-fine effect by 31\% and narrows the gap by 18\%. Freezing the investor population splits the feedback into two channels. In the main setting, investor evolution weakens the information policy ($-0.040$~SD [$-0.054$, $-0.026$], $-12\%$ of its effect), while price and demand responses strengthen it ($+0.032$ [$+0.028$, $+0.037$], $+9.8\%$); the two nearly cancel, and the information policy leaves the impact-investor share 2.0 percentage points lower at the end. The ordering I $>$ F $>$ B holds in 30 of 30 seeds with and without feedback.

The market matters more through the \emph{level} of financing gains it produces than through feedback. The honest summary is that the qualitative results follow from the firms' decision rule, which Section~\ref{sec:benchmark} describes. The artificial market supplies the financing gains that set the magnitudes. Its feedback strengthens fixed fines, narrows the gap between the instruments, and has no identifiable net effect on transparency in the main setting.

\subsection{Learning firms}\label{sec:learning}

We also replaced the NPV rule with tabular Q-learning on realised 60-day accounts: the change in financing fees minus resource costs and fines, scaled by initial value. After fixing what could be fixed --- seven options (three sizes each of improvement and inflation, plus maintaining), polynomial step sizes \citep{evendar2003learning}, a state that carries the firm's own signal tercile, a per-period budget, and contract-scale training worlds --- the learners still failed the pre-set coverage test in the post-policy large-firm group. More importantly, two tables trained independently in the same environment inflated in about 69\% and 15\% of decisions, so the learners' policy ordering cannot be separated from training randomness, and they were excluded from the confirmatory experiment.

A diagnosis with randomly assigned actions points to the size of the signal. At the calibrated premium, the median value of the best action over maintaining is about 0.07 basis points of initial market value (0.52 at the 90th percentile). Within a state, the discounted reward over the following 20 periods, the horizon over which an action's financing effects arrive, has an SD of about 190 basis points. As a rough diagnostic, a two-sample test with that noise would need about $1.2\times10^{8}$ samples per state and action to tell the median value difference apart from zero, and about $2\times10^{6}$ at the 90th percentile, against $10^3$--$10^4$ in our training archives. These figures indicate orders of magnitude; they are not the sample requirement of any particular learning algorithm. Even the one-period reward of maintaining, net of common shocks, has an SD of 2.1 basis points. In addition, the state does not record improvements in progress, so one-step updates credit a delayed financing gain to whatever action happens to be taken when it arrives.

\paragraph{A pre-registered redo.} One hypothesis is that a learner of this kind picks up the quick payoff of inflation more easily than the delayed payoff of improvement, and so drifts towards greenwashing. We tested it in a further pre-registered experiment. The design was written into the research contract (Section~23) and committed (\texttt{8bb5d9e}) before implementation and runs; the frozen configuration (experiment ID \texttt{b4430136\ldots6062}) was committed (\texttt{42ec591}) before the confirmatory run. Every scenario was trained ten times independently on new seeds, in two versions of the state: A, the state above, and V, which replaces the least informative feature, the cost tier, with the firm's cumulative real improvement, including improvement still in progress, so that improvement shows in the state as inflation does. In each replicate a pre-policy table is trained under the baseline, and post-policy tables for B, I, F and IF are trained from it; frozen tables are evaluated on five validation seeds and compared with an NPV firm restricted to the same action grid (discrete NPV). Five primary questions were tested across the ten replicates with the Holm correction. All 540 jobs completed. Two infrastructure interruptions were resumed on the same seeds, and the interrupted attempts are kept in the archive.

\paragraph{Results of the redo.} Coverage still fails: 91 of 100 tables pass the revised test, and all nine failures are post-policy tables, eight of them in the large-firm group, so the acceptance condition for learning firms is still not met. Training randomness dominates: in version A, the SD of the greenwashing share between tables is 14--18 times its SD within a table across validation seeds. One primary question is supported. Under the information policy, learning firms inflate in 23.7\% of decisions against 3.8\% for the discrete NPV firm, a difference of $+19.9$ percentage points [$+7.2$, $+32.6$] (Holm $p = 0.031$). About two thirds of this inflation occurs where the discrete NPV firm would not inflate and would almost always improve instead; such decisions are 15.5\% of all decisions under I. Their median loss against the NPV choice is 0.042 basis points of market value, far below the one-period reward noise of about 2 basis points. This is a trap the learner cannot learn its way out of, not evidence that it has learned that greenwashing pays. The other four questions are unresolved. Making improvement visible lowers the share of such decisions under I from 15.5\% to 9.3\% (Holm $p = 0.47$) and does not significantly change the true change under the baseline ($+0.003$~SD). Learning firms' response to information, $+0.157$~SD [$+0.040$, $+0.274$], is not significant after the correction (Holm $p = 0.057$), and their response to the quadrupled fine is $+0.001$~SD. In an auxiliary, uncorrected comparison, version V greenwashes 34.7 percentage points less than version A on pre-policy tables trained from scratch, but the decisions it saves become maintaining rather than real improvement. When the five replicates that contain a failing table are dropped, all five questions are unresolved, P1 included.

This is a statement about this learner in this calibration, not about real firms. Firms can value ESG through forward-looking appraisal, as our NPV firms do, and through information other than their own accounts. On ten independently trained tables, the redo confirms what one table suggested: learners trained on realised accounts keep inflating where the NPV rule would not, because the loss from doing so is too small to detect. It does not support the mechanism that invisible improvement causes the trap. Because coverage still fails and behaviour depends mainly on which table was trained, learning firms remain outside the main experiment.

\section{Robustness and limitations}\label{sec:validity}
%======================================================================

Appendix~\ref{app:robust} collects the three main contrasts across all 17 settings we ran (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). The ordering I $>$ F $>$ B holds in all of them, and excluding runs that triggered the price guard changes no verdict. The size of the information advantage ranges from $+0.003$ to $+0.224$~SD and depends mainly on two assumptions: that real improvement lasts, and that a fine is charged once per case. A pre-registered test on new seeds that changes both together keeps the ordering in all four settings, with an advantage of $+0.004$ to $+0.012$~SD. The main result is therefore an ordering that is robust across the settings tested, with a magnitude that is not. The remaining limitations are these.

\paragraph{Costs and scope of policy.} Neither the cost of transparency nor that of audits is modelled, so the comparison is not about social cost. In the first experiment the regulator does not choose whom to audit and audit intensity is fixed at 1\% per period; Section~\ref{sec:targeted} tests two targeted rules and a doubled budget. Section~\ref{sec:assurance} adds assurance providers; there are no rating agencies.

\paragraph{The financing channel.} With the calibrated taste, the local response of a firm's financing rate to its signal --- the quantity firms decide on --- is about 2.2 basis points per SD. In our sample of non-financial A-share firms, a one-SD higher Huazheng score goes with a debt cost 28.0 basis points lower in the following year [23.4, 32.5], and 11.9 [7.4, 16.4] within firms; these are associations, not causal effects. Appendix~\ref{app:financing} reports a pre-registered attempt to bring the model to the first estimate. Raising the spread of the financing contract made the contract unstable. A direct channel in which lenders price the rating reached the target and kept every run stable, but most firms then pushed their ratings close to the top of the scale, the rank persistence of ratings fell far below the data, and re-estimating the natural dynamics under the channel found no form that fits. In this model, a financing premium of the estimated size is incompatible with the persistence of Huazheng ratings. Under that channel all eight primary contrasts keep their predicted sign, but the shifts in where greenwashing goes shrink, because almost every decision is then an inflating one. Our magnitudes hold for the smaller financing incentive and should not be extrapolated to one that matches the sample.

\paragraph{Investor response.} The model assumes that investors price reported ESG. In a pre-registered test on the twelve quarterly Huazheng rating updates from January 2023 to October 2025 (Appendix~\ref{app:investors}), two of three primary questions are supported after Holm correction: a one-notch upgrade over the year goes with year-end fund ownership 0.12 percentage points of tradable shares higher [0.07, 0.17], and a one-SD higher score with an implied cost of equity 14.5 basis points lower [3.0, 26.0]. Returns in the two months after an update show no statistically significant response, $+0.14$ percentage points per notch [$-0.17$, $+0.45$]. These are associations, and the fund-ownership result loses statistical significance when the treatment is the change in the normal score or when 2024, the year Huazheng revised its method, is dropped. The model's investors respond more strongly. In the baseline of the re-run with the rating persistence estimated in stage~H, a one-SD change in the signal moves the price by 2.33 percentage points within a quarter, three times the upper end of the data's interval for the same treatment, and over the policy window the slope of dividend yield on the signal is 32.4 basis points per cross-sectional SD, 2.2 times the cost-of-equity estimate; before any policy it is 25.6, inside the interval. The model's prices respond without a release delay, so the first comparison is not like for like. We did not recalibrate the investor taste: in stage~I a single financing target already pushed ratings to the top of the scale (Appendix~\ref{app:financing}), and the taste moves the yield slope, the share of inflating decisions and the rating distribution together. We have not run the main experiments with a weaker taste, so how much the main magnitudes depend on it is not known. Fund-level holdings were not available to us, so the response of ESG funds and the share of impact investors remain untested.

\paragraph{Firm rationality.} Firms are myopic NPV maximisers. Learning firms failed the coverage test in the first comparison and in 9 of 100 tables of the pre-registered redo, and their behaviour depends mainly on which table was trained (Section~\ref{sec:learning}), so the main results say nothing about boundedly rational firms.

\paragraph{Data.} The CSI~300 contains only large firms and is observed at its current composition. A rating is a proxy, not the true attribute, and the date on a rating need not be the date it became public.

\paragraph{Market layer.} In a qualification audit of 60 fixed worlds, 57 passed and 3 failed mechanical checks; 37 confirmatory runs triggered the price guard; and the simulated-moments fit leaves lag-1 return autocorrelation too negative ($-0.18$ against $-0.03$). All are kept in the record, and the main contrasts do not change when the affected runs are excluded.

\section{Targeted audits: a second pre-registered experiment}\label{sec:targeted}
%======================================================================

In every run so far the regulator audits each firm with probability 1\% per period. Two results of the first experiment make the allocation of audits worth testing. The statutory fine is negligible for large firms, which inflate in 66\% of their decisions, and a firm with an open case continues to inflate whatever the fine (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}). Unlike the fine, the audit probability sets both how long inflation survives ($A_w$) and the discounted value of paying on detection ($D$), so it also reaches firms that continue. We therefore ask whether auditing some firms more and others less, at an unchanged expected number of audits, does better than random audits, and whether it matters that firms know the rule.

\paragraph{Design.} The design was written into the research contract (Section~21) and committed (\texttt{962d1ee}) before implementation and runs; the pipeline checks, the frozen configuration (experiment ID \texttt{06bec148\ldots2a32}) and the analysis script were committed (\texttt{3fced38}) before the confirmatory run. The experiment uses 30 new seeds, 42001--42030, and reruns B, I and F on them. On each audit date firm~$i$ is audited if a uniform draw falls below its audit probability $q_i$. The draws are shared across scenarios, and every targeted rule keeps $\sum_i q_i = 2$, the expected number of audits when each of the 200 firms is audited with probability 1\%. The rules are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Random} (B): $q_i = 1\%$.
\item \emph{By value} (V): $q_i$ is proportional to market value. At the initial distribution of values the median small firm is audited with probability 0.41\% per period, the median large firm 3.0\% and the largest firm 8.0\%.
\item \emph{By jump} (J): half the budget is spread evenly ($q_i = 0.5\%$) and the other half is shared among flagged firms, those whose report (true level plus inflation, before the transparency discount) rose by more than $h = 0.125$~SD, half the per-period cap, since the previous audit date. The random half leaves no firm unaudited.
\item \emph{Doubled random audits} (A2): $q_i = 2\%$, twice the budget, as a reference for auditing more.
\end{itemize}
When a rule is public, firms decide with their own audit probability: under V with the day's $q_i$, under J by predicting whether new inflation will be flagged and choosing the better of staying below the threshold and accepting the flag. When it is hidden (\Vh, \Jh), firms decide as in the first experiment, so targeting changes only who is caught. Firms under J ignore the effect of a flag on older undetected inflation, which understates the cost of being flagged and therefore evasion. The 15 scenarios are B, I, F, A2, V, \Vh, J, \Jh{} and VF (value targeting with the fixed fine at four times the statutory maximum) in the main setting, and B, I, V, J, \Jh{} and VF without natural decay. Five primary questions were tested two-sided with the Holm correction (Table~\ref{tab:audit_primary}), and five analytical predictions derived from the propositions were written down with the design; all five held. All 450 runs completed; five triggered the price guard, all on one seed at day 3101 and none in a scenario of the primary questions.

\paragraph{Results.} At an equal number of audits, both targeted rules do worse than random audits when firms know the rule (E1, E2), although both find more: 50\% of audits under V and 69\% under J find inflation, against 28\% under random audits (Table~\ref{tab:audit_describe}). Hidden, neither rule differs significantly from random audits in the equal-weighted true level: \Vh{}~$-$~B is $-0.005$~SD [$-0.012$, $+0.001$] and \Jh{}~$-$~B is $-0.005$ [$-0.011$, $+0.001$]; intervals that include zero do not establish equivalence. The loss therefore appears to come mainly from firms' response to a known rule rather than from detection itself; publishing the jump rule costs $0.131$~SD (E3).

Value targeting moves greenwashing from large to small firms. The share of large firms' decisions that inflate falls from 64\% to 47\%, and that of small firms' decisions rises from 5\% to 35\%. Counted by decisions, small firms make 56.8\% of all inflating decisions under V, against 14.1\% under random audits, and large firms 12.5\% against 31.7\%. The shift also appears without natural decay: inflation is then rarer (5.9\% of decisions under V and 4.7\% under B), but small firms' share of all inflating decisions still rises from 2.7\% to 30.1\%, while the equal-weighted true level is only 0.003~SD lower than under random audits. The size divide reverses: the large-minus-small difference in the change of the true level moves from $-0.30$ to $+0.32$~SD (E4). Weighted by initial market value, the true level rises ($+0.053$~SD [$+0.043$, $+0.063$]), but small firms are 60\% of all firms, and the equal-weighted level falls. Small firms lose more deterrence than large firms gain. The fine is large relative to small firms' value and small relative to large firms', and the detection discount is strongly concave in the audit probability ($D = 0.455$ at 1\% and 0.87 at 8\%). Measured by the expected discounted fixed fine relative to value, with the average firm under random audits at 1, value targeting lowers the median small firm from 2.46 to 1.37 but raises the median large firm only from 0.34 to 0.53. Nor does it end large firms' open cases. By Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold}, with $A_w$ evaluated at the firm's own audit probability, a firm with an open case stops inflating at $\tau = 0.1$ only if that probability is at least 10.5\% per period. All 81,253 continuing inflations under V came from firms below that rate, and the 313 firm-periods that reached it had none.

Firms evade the jump rule. When the rule is public, 44\% of new inflations are flagged at the next audit date, against 54\% when it is hidden (a difference of $-9.6$ percentage points [$-10.9$, $-8.2$]; $-13.8$ points without decay): firms keep inflation below the threshold. Unflagged firms then face an audit probability of only 0.5\%, and small firms, which the fine deterred under random audits, open more new cases: the share of their decisions that inflate rises by 8.7 points against \Jh, and their share of all inflating decisions from 14.6\% to 25.6\%.

Auditing more works where auditing differently does not. Doubling random audits raises the true level by $+0.118$~SD [$+0.103$, $+0.133$] and lowers inflation in every size tier, but large firms still inflate in 54\% of their decisions against 2\% for small firms. Both targeted rules fall far short of it (V~$-$~A2 is $-0.309$~SD and J~$-$~A2 is $-0.254$). Information still beats targeted enforcement with a heavier fine: I~$-$~VF is $+0.156$~SD (E5). Adding value targeting to the quadrupled fine helps little in the equal-weighted level (VF~$-$~F is $+0.034$ [$+0.025$, $+0.044$]) and more when weighted by value ($+0.181$ [$+0.160$, $+0.203$]). On the new seeds the main comparison of the first experiment replicates: I~$-$~F is $+0.191$~SD [$+0.165$, $+0.216$], against $+0.205$ in Table~\ref{tab:primary}. Without natural decay all effects are small and keep their signs (V~$-$~B is $-0.003$~SD, J~$-$~B $-0.008$ and I~$-$~VF $+0.026$), so magnitudes are again read as the range between no decay and 0.2~SD per year (Section~\ref{sec:decay}).

\paragraph{Scope.} The result concerns the two rules tested: a proportional rule, and a jump rule with one threshold, a one-period window and half the budget random. No rule was optimised, so the experiment does not show that targeting cannot beat random audits, only that these two rules do worse once firms know them. The mechanism is reallocation under a fixed budget. Auditing large firms or recent jumps more means auditing small firms or quiet old inflation less, and firms that know the rule inflate where audits thin out. The experiment keeps the other assumptions of the main setting: per-case booking, a common washing cost ratio, uniform decay and fixed doses. The mean-reverting form of Appendix~\ref{app:reversion} was not run.

\section{Assurance providers: a third pre-registered experiment}\label{sec:assurance}
%======================================================================

The information instrument of the first experiment is an abstract transparency $\tau$. One institution that makes inflated reports easier to see through is third-party assurance, and it has a conflict that transparency does not: the firm pays the provider and, unless providers are assigned, chooses it. The 2024 exchange guidelines do not require assurance, although a firm that hires a provider must disclose the provider's independence and qualifications and its assurance report \citep[Art.~58]{sse2024guideline}. Under Article~213(3) of the Securities Law, a securities service provider that fails to exercise due diligence and whose documents contain false records, misleading statements or material omissions forfeits its business income and is fined one to ten times that income, or CNY~0.5--5 million if it has no business income or less than CNY~0.5 million \citep[Art.~213(3)]{securitieslaw2019}. We use the upper limit of that low-income band, CNY~5 million, as the benchmark liability and assume that the provision applies to ESG assurance; we make no judgement on whether it does. We therefore add strategic assurance providers and ask how much assurance does for the true level, and how this depends on the provider's liability, on who chooses the provider and on which firms must be assured.

\paragraph{Design.} The design was written into the research contract (Section~22) and committed (\texttt{307168c}) before implementation and runs; the pipeline checks, the frozen configuration (experiment ID \texttt{35c4f767\ldots339a}) and the analysis script were committed (\texttt{61b1205}) before the confirmatory run. The experiment uses 30 new seeds, 43001--43030, and NPV firms; policy switches at day 1201 as before. Four providers inspect each assured firm once per period, before its report is published. A provider of quality $a_k \in [0, 1]$ finds the inflation in a client's report with probability $a_k$ and corrects all of it before publication; there is no fine and nothing is made public. The firm pays a fee for each inspection. At the start of each assurance year (240 days) each provider sets its quality as a myopic best response, $a_k = \min(1, \lambda s_k)$ with $\lambda = L\bar q/(2\kappa)$. This minimises the effort cost $\kappa a^2$ plus the expected liability, where $s_k$ is the share of its inspections in the previous year that met an inflated report, $\bar q = 1\%$ is the regulator's audit probability, and $L$ is the liability the provider pays when the regulator later detects inflation in a report it inspected. Liability doses are 0, 1, 4, 10, 16 and 100 times the CNY~5 million benchmark. The fee and the effort cost $\kappa$ are assumptions, so doses are read relative to $\kappa$. Providers are either assigned to firms at random (R) or chosen by each firm at the start of every year (F), and assurance is mandatory for all firms (ALL), only for the 20 large firms (LRG), or voluntary (VOL16). The 18 scenarios are B, I, VOL16, ALL\_R0 to ALL\_R100, ALL\_F1, ALL\_F16, LRG\_R16 and LRG\_F16 in the main setting, and five of them without natural decay. From Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold}, a quality of 0.0827 is enough to stop every firm with an interior solution from inflating at the baseline transparency: inspection comes every period and before publication, whereas the regulator audits 1\% of firms per period. Five primary questions were tested two-sided with the Holm correction (Table~\ref{tab:assurance_primary}), and six analytical predictions were written down with the design; all six held. All 540 runs completed. Twenty-nine triggered the price guard; as pre-registered, they remain in the main analysis, and a sensitivity analysis that excludes them leaves all five primary verdicts unchanged.

\paragraph{Results.} With strong liability and assigned providers, assurance works. At 16 times the benchmark liability, ALL\_R16 raises the true level by $+0.273$~SD over the baseline (C1), and the share of inflating decisions falls from 19.0\% to 6.2\%, in all three size tiers. Letting firms choose their provider removes most of that gain: ALL\_F16 is $0.199$~SD below ALL\_R16 (C2), although in scenario means it remains about 0.07~SD above the baseline (Table~\ref{tab:assurance_describe}), and 21.0\% of decisions inflate, slightly more than without assurance. Counted by decisions, 96.9\% of assured firms' inflating decisions (46,489 of 47,953) were made by clients of the year's lowest-quality provider; without natural decay the share is 97.4\% (12,262 of 12,594). The mechanism is an escape cycle. The provider whose clients inflated has a high $s_k$ and so a high quality the next year, and at the start of that year those firms move to a provider whose clients were clean and whose quality is therefore low; about 46 firms switch each year.

At the benchmark liability (ALL\_R1), provider quality averages only 0.014 and the gain is small, $+0.046$~SD (C3). This is the range in which the analytical benchmark predicted that weak assurance acts as insurance against the fine: corrected inflation can no longer be fined, and among firms that have no undetected inflation when they decide, the share of decisions that inflate rises by 0.9 percentage points [$+0.7$, $+1.2$]. The share of all inflating decisions rises from 19.0\% to 20.0\%. The true-level change nevertheless improves relative to the baseline, because improving firms improve by more (0.0229 against 0.0209~SD per improvement on average); this decomposition was made after the results and its mechanism was not tested separately. Against the baseline the dose response is $+0.000$, $+0.046$, $+0.145$, $+0.256$, $+0.273$ and $+0.232$~SD at 0, 1, 4, 10, 16 and 100 times the benchmark. Among the doses tested, 16 times gives the largest gain and 100 times a smaller one; no dose between them was run. At 100 times, quality averages 0.43, yet 8.6\% of decisions inflate, more than at 16 times. The pre-registered prediction of a quality cycle was tested under ALL\_R16. High quality corrects the stock of inflation, so $s_k$ and with it the next year's quality fall and inflation can return. In ALL\_R16 the yearly mean quality first exceeds 0.0827 and later falls below it in 28 of 30 seeds, and some decisions still inflate in the last four assurance years in all 30 seeds. The same feedback may explain the smaller gain at 100 times, but this was not tested separately.

Mandating assurance only for large firms narrows the size divide (LRG\_R16, large minus small $+0.563$~SD; C4). The share of large firms' decisions that inflate falls from 64.7\% to 24.1\%, as when every firm is assured, but that of mid-sized firms' decisions rises from 34.4\% to 38.8\% and that of small firms' from 3.6\% to 6.1\%. Counted by decisions, the firms that need not be assured make 86.4\% of all inflating decisions under LRG\_R16, against 65.9\% without assurance, and the equal-weighted gain is only $+0.057$~SD. Information still beats the assurance setting with the largest gain among those tested (ALL\_R16), by a small margin: I~$-$~ALL\_R16 is $+0.028$~SD (C5), $+0.075$ weighted by value, and $+0.227$ against assurance with firm choice. No firm buys voluntary assurance, so VOL16 is bit-identical to the baseline; investors see only reported scores and do not reward assurance, so this follows from the model's assumptions and does not predict practice. Without natural decay all effects are small and keep their signs ($+0.030$ for C1, $-0.010$ for C2, $+0.004$ for C3 and $+0.009$ for C5), and firm choice still leaves more decisions inflating than no assurance: 5.5\% under ALL\_F16, against 4.9\% without assurance and 2.8\% under ALL\_R16, with about 13 firms switching provider each year, against 46 in the main setting.

\paragraph{Scope.} The strength of assurance comes from its timing: an inspection every period before publication, against regulatory audits of 1\% per period. What limits it is the provider's incentive, set by liability, and who chooses the provider. Providers respond myopically once a year, do not anticipate losing clients, and know the true incidence of inflation in their clients' reports of the previous year, which overstates what they know. The fee and the effort cost are assumptions, and the benchmark liability assumes that Article~213(3) applies to ESG assurance. The model has no provider reputation or exit, no mandatory rotation, no reporting by providers to the regulator and no minimum assurance standard. Nor does it publish whether a firm is assured or the provider's report, although the exchange guidelines require a firm that hires a provider to disclose them; this simplification limits what the voluntary-assurance result says about practice. The result under firm choice suggests that rotation or assignment may matter, but rotation was not tested. The experiment keeps the other assumptions of the main setting: per-case booking, a common washing cost ratio, uniform decay, random audits and a fixed transparency dose. The mean-reverting form was not run.

%======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%======================================================================

Each enforcement instrument we tested leaves greenwashing somewhere to go, and firms find it. A fixed fine leaves it with large firms and with inflation added to cases that are already open. An audit rule that firms know leaves it with the firms the rule audits less, or below the jump the rule looks for. A provider that firms choose leaves it with the laxest provider. These shifts appear with and without natural decay of the true level; what decay changes is how much they cost in true ESG. Transparency leaves less room, because it lowers the return to every inflated report, and at the doses tested it raises firms' true ESG level more than a quadrupled fine. That advantage rests on real improvement lasting: when true levels revert to the mean, it nearly vanishes.

In the model, then, an instrument is better judged by where it pushes greenwashing than by how much it catches. Both targeted rules found inflation more often than random audits and still deterred less once firms knew them. Two of the gaps are questions of law and practice rather than of the model: whether false reports are fined once per case or once per report, and who chooses the assurance provider. How large the advantage of transparency is depends on a third question, whether ESG performance reverts, which verified rating data could settle.

The results are conditional on the model. With the financing contract of the main experiments, firms neither improve nor inflate without investors who value reported ESG, so no instrument has anything to act on; in our sample, fund ownership and the implied cost of equity respond to Huazheng ratings, but less strongly than the model's investors do under policy; firms are myopic NPV maximisers; the costs of transparency and audits are not modelled; and the financing response firms act on is an order of magnitude below the debt-cost association in our sample, which the model cannot match without its ratings bunching at the top of the scale. What the design offers is that each step from assumption to result can be inspected: the analytical benchmark, the feedback decomposition, the pre-registrations and the archive. Verified rating panels, audit rules designed against firms' responses, the rotation and reputation of assurance providers, and firms that learn with enough data are the natural next steps.

\subsection{Mean reversion (exploratory)}\label{app:reversion}

The evidence on decay does not support uniform decay. In a panel of Huazheng ratings of all A-share firms for 2009--2022, which we obtained from a secondary source and have not yet verified against an official export, the average rating has no trend (yearly means between 3.94 and 4.32 on a 1--9 scale), and ratings revert towards the middle: the mean next-quarter change is $+0.30$ notches for the lowest grade and $-0.48$ for the highest. Mean reversion is a different functional form from uniform decay and is not covered by the range above. The panel may not settle the level either: if ratings are relative to peers, an economy-wide trend cannot be seen in them.

We therefore added a mean-reverting form: the true level reverts towards the day's cross-sectional mean at rate $\kappa$ per year, with idiosyncratic shocks that keep the cross-sectional variance at one, and NPV firms account for the reversion when they value improvement. The panel's quarterly autocorrelation of 0.88 implies $\kappa = 0.50$; removing white-noise measurement error with the ratio of the four-quarter to the one-quarter autocorrelation gives $\kappa = 0.32$. We treat both as data-motivated sensitivity values, not as estimates or bounds of how fast the true level reverts: the panel is unverified, and its reversion mixes measurement noise, rating discreteness and firms' own actions, whose net effect on these numbers we cannot sign. Under both rates the ordering I $>$ F $>$ B survives, but the advantage of information almost disappears. The gap between the instruments is $+0.006$~SD [$+0.004$, $+0.008$] at $\kappa = 0.32$ and $+0.003$ [$+0.002$, $+0.004$] at $\kappa = 0.50$, and the effect of information against the baseline is $+0.027$ and $+0.021$. Both lie below the lower end of the uniform-decay range by amounts the paired intervals resolve. The fixed-fine effect, $+0.021$ and $+0.018$, stays inside it. The mechanism is the one Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} predicts. Reversion erodes real improvement (half-lives of 1.4--2.2 years) while inflation is removed only by audits, so $A_r$ falls from 3.73 to 2.01 and 1.51 years, and the threshold at $w = 0.2$ rises to 0.77 and 0.82. Transparency of 0.6 no longer stops inflation: 20--22\% of decisions still inflate under I, against 25--28\% under B, so transparency acts much like a heavier fine. Reversion is thus not a decay rate between the two ends of the range. Like no decay, it does not pull the average down; like strong decay, it makes room for inflation; and it lowers the value of real improvement.

\subsection{A fine for every false report (exploratory)}\label{app:perreport}

When every false report is fined separately from day~0, and NPV firms charge each new inflation its expected fine, the share of inflating decisions falls from 14.3\% at the statutory fine to 6.6\%, 2.4\% and 0.8\% at 2, 4 and 8 times it. Under per-case booking the corresponding shares are 22.3\%, 15.5\%, 13.3\% and 12.5\%. Deterrence still diminishes: each doubling adds $+0.094$, $+0.064$ and $+0.034$~SD, and each step is smaller than the last by an amount the paired intervals resolve. It diminishes because each doubling deters fewer and larger firms, for which the fine is a smaller share of value; the remaining inflation comes from the largest firms. The fine becomes a stronger instrument ($+0.158$~SD against the baseline, from $+0.125$), the baseline itself improves, and information is unaffected because it stops inflation outright. The gap between information and the quadrupled fine therefore shrinks from $+0.205$ to $+0.058$~SD [$+0.042$, $+0.074$], still positive. Which booking is right is a legal question: several years of false disclosure are often handled in a single penalty decision, but a separate fine per false report is also a plausible reading of the law.

\subsection{Firm-specific washing costs (exploratory)}\label{app:hetero}

We drew each firm's ratio $w_i$ from a lognormal distribution with mean 0.2 and log-SD $\ln 2$ (5th to 95th percentile 0.05 to 0.49). Inflation then no longer stops at a common threshold. At $\tau = 0.6$, 7.3\% of decisions still inflate (none with a common ratio), in every seed. At $\tau = 0.8$, 0.4\% do, in 29 of 30 seeds. The decline is governed firm by firm: all 53,015 inflating decisions after the switch, across the three transparency levels, came from firms whose own threshold $\tau^*(w_i)$ exceeded the current $\tau$, as Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} implies. The policy comparison barely moves. Information beats the baseline by $+0.320$~SD (common ratio $+0.331$) and the fine by $+0.202$ ($+0.205$). The plateau above $\tau = 0.6$ in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} partly reflects the common ratio: with dispersed ratios, raising transparency from 0.6 to 0.8 still cuts inflation from 7.3\% to 0.4\%.

\subsection{Both assumptions together (pre-registered)}\label{app:joint}

Both assumptions narrow the gap, mean reversion by weakening transparency and per-report fines by strengthening the fixed fine, so together they could have let the quadrupled fine catch up with transparency. It did not, because a fine for every report also applies under I, which keeps the baseline fine: inflation under I falls from 18--21\% of decisions with reversion alone to 0.8--1.3\%, and under F to 2.2--2.4\%. Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} implies the same ordering of who inflates: with a fine per report, a firm that inflates under I in a given state would also inflate under F, as long as its choice is not at the per-period cap. The advantage of transparency is $+0.012$~SD [$+0.009$, $+0.015$] at $\kappa = 0.32$ and $+0.006$ [$+0.005$, $+0.008$] at $\kappa = 0.50$; firm-specific washing costs lower it by about 0.002. The reference blocks of the same run reproduce the earlier exploratory values on the new seeds (main setting $+0.200$, fine per report $+0.067$, reversion alone $+0.006$ and $+0.002$).

\paragraph{What remains untested.} The mean-reversion rates are data-motivated sensitivity values from an unverified panel, not estimates or bounds; rates between 0 and 0.32, reversion combined with decay, and firms that ignore reversion were not run. The per-report booking was tested only in the main setting and together with mean reversion.

\subsection{An in-sample financing premium (pre-registered)}\label{app:financing}

\paragraph{Estimate.} Before estimating, we fixed the sample, variables and specification in the research contract (\S26). In a panel of non-financial A-share firms, we regress the debt cost in year $t+1$ (interest expense over average interest-bearing debt) on the year-$t$ Huazheng composite score, converted to a normal score within each rating date, with controls for size, leverage, profitability and state ownership, industry-by-year fixed effects and standard errors clustered by firm. The estimate was run once. On 41,662 firm-years of 4,706 firms (2009--2024), a one-SD higher score goes with a debt cost 28.0 basis points lower [23.4, 32.5]. In fourteen of fifteen robustness specifications the estimate is positive and its interval excludes zero; the thirteen of them measured per SD range from 11.9 to 36.2 basis points, and with firm fixed effects the estimate is 11.9 [7.4, 16.4]. The exception is the subsample of current CSI~300 constituents, $-0.6$ [$-15.8$, $+14.6$], indistinguishable from zero. Bond issue spreads point the same way, 6.8 [2.4, 11.3]. These are associations, not causal effects; if part of the association reflects firm quality correlated with the rating, the model overstates firms' incentive. By coincidence the estimate is close to the cost-of-equity association of about 28 basis points per SD that the investor taste targets \citep{zhao2024esg}; the two are different quantities.

\paragraph{Mapping into the model.} The model's counterpart is $\rho$, the local response of a firm's financing rate to its own signal, about 2.2 basis points per SD. A first pre-registered rule raised only the contract spread, to 0.606 from 0.05. This also raised the level of the contract rate, to 22.7\% at the start, and 37 of 42 development runs at that spread failed, 36 of them because financing charges exhausted firm value. No confirmatory run was started. The amended rule keeps the spread and lets the debt rate respond directly to the rating:
\[
r_i = r_f + s\,\frac{E_i}{E_i+P_i} - \lambda\,(z_i - \bar z),
\]
where $z_i$ is the latest public signal and $\bar z$ its equal-weighted cross-sectional mean on the quote date. With $\lambda^* = 25.75$ basis points per unit of the signal, the median pre-policy response is 28.1 basis points. The model counts one unit of the signal as one SD, while the cross-sectional SD of signals is about 0.67, so per cross-sectional SD the channel is about 17 basis points. The channel prices the rating relative to other firms: the equal-weighted mean of the direct term is zero on every quote date, but the exposure-weighted mean is not.

\paragraph{Confirmatory re-run.} We re-ran the main, targeted-audit and assurance scenarios at $\lambda^*$ with the rating persistence estimated in stage~H, together with a sensitivity block at four other values of $\lambda$ including $\lambda = 0$: 1,590 runs on new seeds, all completed. All eight Holm-corrected primary questions keep their predicted sign and are distinguishable from zero (Table~\ref{tab:financing}). The contrasts in where greenwashing goes shrink sharply. At $\lambda^*$, 93.5\% of baseline decisions are inflating ones, against 21.4\% at $\lambda = 0$; within each size class the rate is 93--95\%, so each class's share of inflating decisions is close to its fixed share of all decisions, and no instrument has much room to shift it. Contrasts in the true level partly grow and partly shrink. Across the five values of $\lambda$, all 40 uncorrected intervals exclude zero with the predicted sign; most of the change in magnitude occurs between $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda = 9.7$ basis points.

\paragraph{Rating persistence under the channel.} The natural dynamics used in the re-run were estimated at $\lambda = 0$. Replaying the baseline at $\lambda^*$ shows that the rank autocorrelation of ratings falls from 0.93 to 0.67 at a lag of one quarter and from 0.55 to 0.05 at twelve quarters, against 0.93 and 0.55 in the data, far outside the data's sampling error. A pre-registered re-estimation of the natural dynamics at $\lambda^*$ found that none of the three forms fits; the best, uniform decay of 0.15~SD per year, has a fit statistic of 497, against 39 for the form selected at $\lambda = 0$, and a pre-set rule stopped the confirmatory re-run at the new estimate. The reason is that at $\lambda^*$ most firms push their reported signals close to the top of the scale. In the last quarter under the re-estimated dynamics, the middle half of firms lies within a band 0.11 wide, against 1.43 at $\lambda = 0$, so ratings no longer separate firms and their ranks are fragile to small measurement errors.

\paragraph{Reading.} In this model, a direct rating response of the estimated size makes most firms bunch at the top of the scale, which is incompatible with the persistence of Huazheng ratings. This is a statement about the model, with its cap on actions per period, its ceiling on reports and its reduced-form contract; it does not say that real firms have no financing premium. We therefore keep the smaller financing incentive for the main results and report the re-run beside them: the signs of the eight contrasts do not depend on $\lambda$, but their magnitudes do, and should not be extrapolated to a financing incentive that matches the sample. Under the direct channel, firms' financing incentive no longer depends on investors' taste, so the zero-taste control of the main experiments applies to the original contract only.

\paragraph{Results.} Two of the three questions are supported (Table~\ref{tab:investors}). A one-notch upgrade over the year goes with year-end fund ownership 0.12 percentage points of tradable shares higher, against a mean ownership of 5.1\% and a standard deviation of the yearly change of 5.3 points. A one-SD change in the public signal moves $\log(P/V)$ by 2.33 percentage points within a quarter [2.13, 2.54], against $+0.12$ [$-0.52$, $+0.77$] per SD of the change in the normal score in the data. \paragraph{Reading.} Investors in our sample do respond to Huazheng ratings through fund ownership and the cost of equity, which supports the direction of the model's central assumption; the response is smaller than the model's under policy, and the return response is not distinguishable from zero. We did not change the investor taste, for the reasons given in Section~\ref{sec:validity}.
