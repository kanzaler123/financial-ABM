\begin{abstract}
\noindent Two public audit rules in a simulated disclosure market detect inflated reports in a higher share of inspections than random auditing, even as firms inflate their reports more often.
The pre-registered experiments hold the regulator's expected audit budget fixed in a market with 200 firms and 1,000 investors. Chinese market data inform initial firm sizes and ratings.
In the reference setting, targeting by market value raises the audit hit rate from 28\% to 50\% and shifts misreporting towards smaller firms.
Targeting jumps in reported scores raises the hit rate to 69\%, while firms keep more of their new inflation below the threshold.
Relative to random audits, equal-weighted true environmental, social and governance (ESG) performance falls by 0.191 standard deviations under value targeting and 0.136 under jump targeting.
When the rules are hidden and firms assume uniform audit risk, both differences are close to zero.
In a separate experiment, firms under mandatory assurance can switch when their provider tightens scrutiny. With provider liability at 16 times the benchmark, clients of the year's least stringent provider account for 96.9\% of assured firms' decisions to inflate.
Across 20 three-policy comparisons, including replications with fresh seeds, greater transparency ranks first on equal-weighted true ESG, a quadrupled fixed fine second and baseline enforcement third. The transparency advantage depends on how long real improvements persist.
A separate behavioural check using account-level tabular Q-learning fell short of its pre-specified state--action coverage target, leaving the policy comparisons conditional on forward-looking firm decisions.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} multi-agent systems; normative enforcement; compliance arbitrage; inspection games; strategic auditing; multi-agent reinforcement learning; agent-based market simulation; pre-registration.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

When normative enforcement relies on imperfect monitoring and bounded sanctions, self-interested agents rarely respond to stricter oversight simply by complying more; they shift misconduct toward whichever institutional margin remains weakest. We investigate this cross-agent compliance-arbitrage mechanism in sustainability (ESG) disclosure through a sequence of pre-registered experiments in a coupled four-party multi-agent system initialised on the scale and rating distribution of China's CSI~300 index. Within one unified market architecture, 1,000 profit-maximising and impact investors trade shares and evolve by pairwise imitation, 200 size-heterogeneous firms choose every 60 trading days between costly real improvement, cheap report inflation and maintaining, a regulator sets disclosure transparency, fines and audit allocation rules, and four strategic third-party assurance providers inspect client reports before publication. Because neither large-scale transparency reforms nor adversarial enforcement rules have been observed in isolation---and underlying true ESG performance is unobservable in field data---controlled multi-agent simulation provides a direct laboratory for institutional comparison.

We position this paper explicitly as a \emph{mechanism and institutional design study initialised on real market scales}, not as an empirical effect-size forecast for Chinese ESG regulation. That positioning is dictated by our own pre-registered empirical checks (Section~\ref{sec:regimes}). While institutional scales are anchored in CSI~300 firm values, Huazheng composite ratings and China's statutory disclosure penalties, formal minimum-distance estimation against the 2009--2021 Huazheng rating panel rejects uniform natural decay ($\delta = 0.2$~SD/year; $T = 2{,}573, p < 10^{-6}$) alongside pure mean reversion and permanent-plus-transitory dynamics, and scaling financing incentives to sample debt-cost associations drives near-universal inflation and collapses rank persistence. Consequently, absolute effect sizes in standard deviations are sensitive to persistence and financing regimes. The targeted-audit experiments trace shifts in misreporting across firm sizes and reporting thresholds, and the assurance experiments trace shifts across verification providers. A separate set of comparisons ranks the three fixed policies on equal-weighted true ESG (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). We therefore treat persistence dynamics and financing-incentive intensity not as footnotes, but as explicit structural phase boundaries in the main analysis (Section~\ref{sec:regimes}).

The institutional motivation is concrete in China, where exchange guidelines mandate sustainability reports for several hundred large listed firms starting with the 2025 reporting year but provide only disciplinary sanctions \citep[e.g.][]{sse2024guideline}, leaving open whether the Securities Law's CNY~10 million cap per false-disclosure case \citep[Art.~197]{securitieslaw2019}---about 0.01\% of median CSI~300 market value---reaches ESG reports. In our high-headroom reference regime ($\delta = 0.2$), statutory fixed fines leave large firms inflating in 66\% of their decisions against 6\% for small firms, and raising the fine eightfold still leaves 12.5\% of decisions inflating because per-case booking adds no new fixed fine when inflation is appended to an existing undetected case (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Yet the paper's core multi-agent findings go well beyond this single-firm static incentive rule.

A natural objection to agent-based market models is whether an artificial stock market adds anything beyond a single-agent analytical formula. In Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}, we deliberately derive two single-firm propositions (Propositions~\ref{prop:fixed}--\ref{prop:threshold}) to mark the exact boundary of what static best-response reasoning can predict, and we organise the paper around three layers of coupled multi-agent dynamics that static single-firm equations cannot capture:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Endogenous market feedback and channel cancellation (Section~\ref{sec:feedback}).} Exact counterfactual replay separates single-firm NPV optimisation from market-level feedback. Although net feedback on the transparency policy appears near zero ($-0.008$~SD), freezing investor evolution exposes two statistically distinct opposing channels---investor outflows weaken transparency by $-12.1\%$ ($-0.040$~SD), while price--demand adjustments strengthen it by $+9.8\%$ ($+0.032$~SD)---and replaying firms on a static market snapshot distorts policy magnitudes by 27--71\%.
\item \emph{Adversarial reallocation and evasion under public audit heuristics (Section~\ref{sec:targeted}).} Holding the expected audit budget fixed, two canonical public targeting rules---auditing in proportion to market value (V) and flagging single-period score jumps (J)---both achieve far higher audit hit rates than random audits (50\% and 69\% vs.\ 28\%) yet perform \emph{worse} on equal-weighted true ESG ($-0.191$ and $-0.136$~SD in the reference regime). Value targeting displaces greenwashing to small firms (raising their share of inflating decisions from 14.1\% to 56.8\% in the reference regime, and by $+24.3$~percentage points under estimated persistence $Q$); jump targeting induces threshold evasion (flag rates fall from 54\% to 44\% once public). Hiding either rule restores near-parity with random audits, confirming that the loss arises from strategic firm--regulator coupling rather than detection mechanics.
\item \emph{Emergent firm--assurer co-evolution (Section~\ref{sec:assurance}).} Introducing reactive third-party assurance providers who update pre-publication inspection quality $a_k$ annually generates genuinely cross-agent emergent dynamics. Under firm choice, an annual \emph{escape cycle} emerges: providers whose clients inflated raise $a_k$ the next year, prompting about 46 firms per year to switch to cleaner, laxer providers; 96.9\% of assured firms' inflating decisions concentrate at the year's laxest provider (95.6\% under $Q$, where Shapley decomposition attributes $+15.3$~pp to client reallocation and $+54.4$~pp to within-client behavioural amplification), wiping out most of mandatory assurance's gain ($-0.199$~SD against random assignment). Even under random provider assignment, endogenous \emph{quality cycles} arise as high quality clears undetected inflation, reduces next-year $s_k$ and drops quality back below the stopping threshold (28 of 30 seeds), producing a striking \emph{non-monotonic liability response} in which $100\times$ statutory liability underperforms $16\times$ ($+0.232$ vs.\ $+0.273$~SD; 8.6\% vs.\ 6.2\% inflating).
\end{enumerate}
Disclosure transparency ($\tau$) is harder to route around because it discounts every inflated report regardless of firm size, case history, audit schedule or provider choice. At the fixed doses tested (not matched on implementation cost), raising $\tau$ from 0.1 to 0.6 outperforms quadrupling the fixed fine across the 20 specifications and replications in Table~\ref{tab:robust}, though its true-ESG magnitude exhibits a sharp phase boundary governed by improvement persistence (Section~\ref{sec:regimes}): when deliberate improvement fades as fast as transitory shocks under form~$Q$, $\tau^*(0.2)$ rises from 0.57 to 0.825 $> 0.6$, shrinking the transparency advantage from $+0.205$~SD to $+0.003$~SD ($+0.077$~SD when deliberate improvement is permanent).

Our analysis of enforcement in multi-agent systems (MAS) connects normative MAS and electronic-institution enforcement \citep{boella2006introduction,arcos2005engineering,balke2013iabm,dellanna2020runtime}, strategic audit allocation \citep{tambe2011security,blocki2015audit} and agent-based green disclosure \citep{zhao2025agent,zhao2026green}. Whereas \citet{zhao2025agent,zhao2026green} model greenwashing as passive pooling across three discrete green tiers without audits, sanctions or assurance providers, our system models active continuous misreporting, size-heterogeneous sanction deterrence, public versus hidden audit rules, and co-evolving assurance providers coupled to an artificial equity market. To test these mechanisms, we incorporate formal pre-analysis plans \citep{olken2015promises} into agent-based institutional design: every confirmatory block---1,770 markets in Study~1 (policy instruments), 450 in Study~2 (targeted audits), 540 in Study~3 (third-party assurance), 540 jobs in the learning-firm replication, 720 in the joint robustness test, 720 in the rating-persistence re-run, and 1,590 in the financing-channel study---had frozen configuration hashes, fresh paired seed blocks, Holm-corrected primary hypotheses and full archival of failed runs, complemented by exact counterfactual replay decomposition. To examine the assumption that firms value actions by their expected net present value, we also test tabular Q-learning on firms' realised accounts (Section~\ref{sec:learning}). The median single-decision advantage is 0.07 basis points against a 20-period discounted reward standard deviation of 190 basis points. This behavioural check falls short of its pre-specified state--action coverage target, leaving the policy comparisons conditional on forward-looking firms.

\section{Related literature}\label{sec:literature}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{Normative multi-agent systems, electronic institutions and compliance enforcement.}
A central thread in MAS studies how institutional norms, monitors and sanctions steer autonomous self-interested agents toward system-level objectives \citep{boella2006introduction}. Electronic institutions formalise interaction protocols and enforcement infrastructure in open agent societies \citep{arcos2005engineering}, while institutional agent-based modelling couples formal norm frameworks with population simulation to test enforcement policies before deployment \citep{balke2013iabm}. When monitoring is imperfect or partial, designers must reason explicitly about what violations can actually be observed and sanctioned \citep{alechina2014norm}; game-theoretic norm-based mechanism design characterises when sanctions can implement desirable equilibria \citep{bulling2016norm}, and runtime sanction revision adapts penalties online from observed compliance traces \citep{dellanna2020runtime}. Parallel work in law and economics establishes that deterrence depends on expected penalties \citep{becker1968crime}, that penalties invariant to offence scale erode marginal deterrence \citep{stigler1970optimum,mookherjee1994marginal}, and that inspection games capture strategic monitor--inspectee interaction \citep{avenhaus2002inspection}, with corporate fraud detection estimated at roughly one third \citep{dyck2024pervasive}. Our model advances normative MAS enforcement by embedding sanction booking (per-case vs.\ per-report), imperfect inspection, delegated verification (assurance providers) and disclosure transparency inside a market-clearing economy where heterogeneous agents exploit gaps across institutional margins rather than responding to a single isolated norm.

\paragraph{Stackelberg security games and strategic audit allocation.}
Algorithmic game theory and MAS research on Stackelberg Security Games (SSGs) studies how a resource-constrained defender should commit to a randomized inspection or patrol schedule when a strategic adversary observes the commitment before acting \citep{conitzer2006computing,kiekintveld2009computing,tambe2011security,sinha2018stackelberg}. \citet{blocki2015audit} extend this framework directly to \emph{audit games}, jointly optimizing randomized inspection schedules and penalty levels under bounded defender resources. Our targeted-audit experiment (Section~\ref{sec:targeted}) connects directly to this tradition while addressing a complementary institutional question: rather than computing a Stackelberg-optimal dynamic audit schedule, we evaluate the \emph{adversarial failure of two canonical public heuristics} commonly proposed in financial supervision---allocating audits proportionally to firm market value or triggering inspections on single-period score jumps---against both uniform random audits and hidden-rule counterfactuals. Comparing public and hidden rules isolates the exact commitment-response loss predicted by security-game theory when a non-optimal rule is made observable to strategic inspectees.

\paragraph{Agent-based models of green disclosure and empirical game-theoretic analysis.}
The closest MAS studies to ours are \citet{zhao2025agent} and \citet{zhao2026green}, who build agent-based models of green disclosure policies (extending to an EU--China dual-market setting in the journal version) in which firms at three discrete green levels compete for ESG fund capital. They evaluate disclosure policies using empirical game-theoretic analysis (EGTA; \citealp{wellman2025empirical}) combined with $\alpha$-Rank \citep{omidshafiei2019alpharank} to identify which strategy profiles persist in stationary evolutionary distributions under strict, lax and two-stage disclosure regimes. In their framework, firms decide only whether to upgrade, greenwashing arises \emph{passively} when lax disclosure pools low- and high-green firms at their average valuation, there are no audits, fines or assurance providers, and firms do not differ in market scale. Our work complements and extends theirs in four respects: (i) firms actively choose continuous report inflation under explicit cost and detection risk; (ii) enforcement includes statutory fixed fines booked per case versus per report, proportional fines, public/hidden targeted audits and strategic third-party assurance providers; (iii) calibrated size heterogeneity interacts with lump-sum penalties to generate cross-tier displacement; and (iv) financing payoffs emerge from an order-driven equity market with evolving investors, enabling exact counterfactual decomposition of market feedback (Section~\ref{sec:feedback}). Methodologically, while EGTA with $\alpha$-Rank identifies evolutionary stability over coarse meta-strategies, our pre-registered paired-seed simulation protocol tests directional and mechanistic hypotheses across rich continuous-state institutional designs and preserves negative results alongside confirmatory successes.

\paragraph{Multi-agent learning, credit assignment and agent-based market methodology.}
Learning in multi-agent environments faces dual barriers of non-stationarity---as co-evolving agents shift each other's transition and payoff dynamics---and credit assignment across noisy, delayed rewards \citep{shoham2007multiagent,busoniu2008comprehensive,hernandezleal2019survey}. \citet{agogino2008analyzing} formally demonstrate how low reward signal-to-noise ratios cripple multi-agent reinforcement learners in stochastic domains and motivate variance-reduced difference rewards, while standard RL methods rely on baselines and control variates to reduce gradient and value-estimation variance \citep{sutton2018reinforcement}. Section~\ref{sec:learning} contributes an explicit quantitative diagnosis of this barrier in an artificial stock market, showing that unmodified account-level tabular Q-learning \citep{watkins1992qlearning,evendar2003learning} cannot resolve 0.07~bp action advantages buried inside 190~bp multi-period financial return variance. Our market microstructure builds on artificial stock markets with heterogeneous adaptive agents \citep{arthur1997asset,lebaron2006agent,farmer2009economy}, demand-system elasticities \citep{koijen2019demand}, and ODD-style structural transparency \citep{grimm2020odd}. To curb researcher degrees of freedom in complex agent-based simulations, we adopt pre-analysis plans \citep{olken2015promises} via version-controlled research contracts committed before confirmatory runs.

\paragraph{Empirical greenwashing, regulatory arbitrage and sustainable finance.}
Our institutional mechanisms draw on empirical and theoretical work in economics and finance. \citet{lyon2011greenwash} model greenwashing as selective disclosure under activist audit threats \citep[see also][]{delmas2011drivers,lyon2015means,kim2015greenwash,marquis2016scrutiny}; mandatory CSR disclosure studies document real effects \citep{christensen2021mandatory,chen2018effect,fiechter2022real}, while rating divergence across providers underscores why reported ESG scores remain noisy proxies \citep{berg2022aggregate}. Empirical studies repeatedly find misconduct shifting into enforcement gaps: pollution rises on unmonitored days \citep{zou2021unwatched}, concentrates downstream of water monitors \citep{he2020watering} or drops once monitoring is automated \citep{greenstone2022technology}, and relocates across jurisdictions or ownership boundaries \citep{bartram2022real,duchin2025sustainability}. Client-paid auditors bias reports just below compliance cutoffs \citep{duflo2013truth} and issuer choice enables rating shopping \citep{skreta2009ratings,bolton2012credit}, whereas third-party information trails curtail evasion where audits alone fail \citep{kleven2011unwilling,pomeranz2015taxation}. Finally, equilibrium models of sustainable investing show that investor taste lowers green firms' cost of capital \citep{heinkel2001effect,pastor2021sustainable,pedersen2021responsible}, with empirical estimates spanning US bank loans \citep{goss2011impact} and Chinese equity costs \citep{zhao2024esg,easton2004pe}; in our market, that same financing premium rewards real improvement and undetected inflation alike.

\subsection{Unified four-party system and timing}

A simulation runs for 3,500 trading days (252 per year, about 14 years). Day~0 initialises the market; days 1--1200 form a pre-policy period under the baseline regime; a policy regime, if any, switches on at day 1201 and remains in place. Outcomes are measured over the policy window, days 1200--3500. The unified multi-agent architecture comprises four strategic or adaptive agent populations interacting through an order-driven equity market: 1,000 heterogeneous investors (profit-maximising and impact types evolving by pairwise imitation), 200 firms in three size tiers (the largest 10\%, the next 30\% and the remaining 60\% by initial market value), a regulator (setting disclosure transparency, fines, audit allocation rules and assurance liability), and---when delegated verification is active---four strategic third-party assurance providers, all coupled via market makers with an aggregate noise-trader account.

Figure~\ref{fig:schematic} presents the integrated system. Rather than treating baseline enforcement, targeted audits and third-party assurance as separate models, all three studies activate modular institutional configurations within this single architecture: Study~1 (Section~\ref{sec:results}) activates the core investor--market--firm--regulator loop under uniform random audits; Study~2 (Section~\ref{sec:targeted}) replaces uniform audits with public or hidden targeted audit rules (V, J) to study strategic regulator--firm coupling; and Study~3 (Section~\ref{sec:assurance}) activates the four reactive assurance providers to study coupled firm--assurer co-evolution. Table~\ref{tab:parameters} lists the core parameters, and Appendix~\ref{app:formal} collects the full closed-form specification and microstructure parameter values.

\subsection{Firms and the disclosure state}

Each firm $i$ has a latent true level $g_i$, measured in units of its initial cross-sectional standard deviation (SD), and undetected inflation $x_i \ge 0$; its report is $g_i + x_i$. Investors observe the signal
\begin{equation}
s_i = \min\{\, g_i + (1-\tau)\,x_i,\; C \,\},
\label{eq:signal}
\end{equation}
where $\tau \in [0,1]$ is transparency, the share of inflation that investors see through, and $C$ is the latent value of a perfect score of 100 points. Reports cannot exceed a perfect score, so inflation is capped at $\max\{C - g_i, 0\}$. In the high-headroom reference regime of Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:assurance}, the true level decays without effort by $\delta = 0.2$~SD per year; Section~\ref{sec:regimes} systematically varies $\delta \in [0, 0.2]$ and replaces uniform decay with pure mean reversion and permanent-plus-transitory dynamics $Q$ estimated against Huazheng rating autocorrelations.

Every 60 trading days each firm takes one of three actions. \emph{Improve} by $\Delta$ costs $c_i\Delta^2$ and raises $g_i$ after an implementation delay of 120 days. \emph{Inflate} by $\Delta$ costs $w\,c_i\Delta^2$ and enters the published report after 20 days. \emph{Maintain} costs nothing. Changes are capped at 0.25~SD per period. Improvement beyond the report's remaining room replaces inflation, so only its transparent share is newly visible. The marginal cost scale is $c_i = \bar c\, m_{k(i)}\, \varepsilon_i\, e^{\eta g_i}$, with $\bar c = 0.05$, size multipliers $m = (0.6, 1, 1.6)$ for large, mid and small firms, lognormal dispersion $\varepsilon_i$ with log-SD 0.5, and $\eta = 1.5$, so improvement becomes dearer as the level rises. The size multipliers assume that large firms improve more cheaply; an equal-cost control removes them. The washing cost ratio $w = 0.2$ has no empirical basis and is varied in the experiment. Costs, fines and subsidies change a firm's intrinsic value and reach its price through the market makers on the next day.

\subsection{Investors and prices}\label{sec:market}

Investors differ in wealth (lognormal), risk aversion $\gamma_j$ and decision calendar (median 20 days). On a decision day an investor sets its equity share to the Merton ratio $(E[R_m]-r_f)/(\gamma_j \sigma_m^2)$, clipped to $[0,1]$, and allocates equity across firms by a softmax tilt away from market weights,
\begin{equation}
w_{ji} \propto P_i \exp(\kappa_j U_{ji}), \qquad U_{ji} = E[R_i] - \gamma_j\,\mathrm{cov}(r_i, r_m) + \theta_j\, s_i ,
\label{eq:utility}
\end{equation}
moving half-way from its current to its target holdings. Profit-maximising investors (PM) have $\theta_j = 0$. Impact investors (II) have a lognormal taste $\theta_j$, measured as an annual-return equivalent per SD of the signal, with median $\bar\theta$ and log-SD 0.5. With equal utilities every investor holds the market portfolio, which keeps the rule consistent with a fixed supply of shares.

Each firm $i$ has unit share supply (so its price $P_{i,t}$ equals its market capitalisation) and is quoted by a market maker who passes through intrinsic-value news $\Delta\ln V_{i,t}$ and moves prices with net order flow $Q^{\text{net}}_{i,t}$ (from deciding investors plus an aggregate AR(1) noise-trader account with volatility-scaled participation; Appendix~\ref{app:formal}). Let $v_{i,t}$ and $v^{\text{ref}}_{i,t}$ denote fast ($\alpha_{\text{var}} = 0.94$) and slow ($\alpha_{\text{ref}} = 0.995$) EWMA squared log-return variances. Volatility stress $\text{stress}_{i,t} = \operatorname{clip}\big(\sqrt{v_{i,t}/v^{\text{ref}}_{i,t}} - 0.8,\; 0,\; 1\big)$ thins the depth fraction $\phi_{i,t}$ toward $\phi^*_{i,t} = \max\big(0.3,\; \exp(-3.03\,\text{stress}_{i,t})\big)$ via $\phi_{i,t} = \max\big(0.3,\; \phi_{i,t-1} + 0.15(\phi^*_{i,t} - \phi_{i,t-1})\big)$. Defining reference depth $L^{\text{ref}}_{i,t} = 0.3\,P_{i,t-1}$ and normalised flow impact $f_{i,t} = (Q^{\text{net}}_{i,t} P_{i,t-1}) / L^{\text{ref}}_{i,t}$, permanent impact scales with $L^{\text{ref}}_{i,t}$ while transient impact reacts to flow surprises scaled by thinned depth $\phi_{i,t}$:
\begin{align}
u_{i,t} &= \frac{f_{i,t}}{\phi_{i,t}} - \bar f_{i,t-1}, \qquad \bar f_{i,t} = 0.3\,\bar f_{i,t-1} + 0.7\,\frac{f_{i,t}}{\phi_{i,t}}, \nonumber\\
T_{i,t} &= 0.974\,T_{i,t-1} + (1 - 0.486)\,u_{i,t}, \label{eq:impact}\\
\Delta^{\text{raw}}_{i,t} &= 0.9\,\Delta\ln V_{i,t} + 0.486\,f_{i,t} + (T_{i,t} - T_{i,t-1}) + 0.01\,(I^*_i - I_{i,t-1}), \label{eq:rawmove}\\
\Delta\ln P_{i,t} &= \operatorname{clip}\!\big(\Delta^{\text{raw}}_{i,t},\; -0.3,\; +0.3\big), \qquad P_{i,t} = P_{i,t-1}\exp(\Delta\ln P_{i,t}), \label{eq:priceguard}
\end{align}
where $I^*_i - I_{i,t-1}$ is the market maker's inventory gap. Seven microstructure parameters ($\sigma_V = 0.159$, $\beta_{\text{liq}} = 3.03$, $\eta_{\text{perm}} = 0.486$, $\delta_{\text{trans}} = 0.974$, $\sigma_{\text{noise}} = 0.002$, $\rho_{\text{noise}} = 0.80$, $\nu_{\text{noise}} = 1.77$; complete values in Appendix~\ref{app:formal}, Table~\ref{tab:microstructure}) were fitted by the method of simulated moments to US industry portfolio returns and frozen before any policy experiment.

Two numerical accounting details warrant explicit disclosure. First, while investors and the noise-trader account are strictly short-sale- and cash-constrained (buys are scaled down proportionally so post-trade investor cash stays nonnegative), the market maker absorbs all residual executed flow: aggregate cash (initial cash plus cumulative dividends) and shares (1 per firm) are conserved every day to tolerance $10^{-8}$, \emph{but the market maker's cash balance is permitted to turn negative with no borrowing ceiling}. Conservation checks therefore establish exact accounting closure, not dealer solvency or liquidity-crisis defaults. Second, equation~\eqref{eq:priceguard} defines the \emph{numerical price guard}: a mechanical validation gate flags any simulation in which at least one firm on at least one day hits the single-day log-return clamp $|\Delta^{\text{raw}}_{i,t}| > 0.3$ (a one-day simple price move beyond $-25.9\%$ or $+35.0\%$). A separate hard divergence abort halts execution if $|\ln(P_{i,t}/V_{i,t})| > 5.0$, which never occurred in any confirmatory run. Guarded runs are kept in the primary statistics and sensitivity-checked by exclusion.

\subsection{Investor evolution}

Every 60 days, 10\% of investors review their type. Each reviewer samples one other investor uniformly; if the partner is of the other type, the reviewer switches with probability $k \cdot \max\{0, \bar G_{\text{other}} - \bar G_{\text{own}}\}$, where $\bar G$ is the mean wealth growth of each type over the window and $k = 100$. This is the proportional imitation rule of \citet{schlag1998imitate}. The expected change in the impact-investor share $s$ is $m\,k\,s(1-s)\,E[\bar G_{II} - \bar G_{PM}]$ with review share $m$, a discrete replicator dynamic \citep{taylor1978evolutionary}, so the share does not drift when the two types perform equally. An earlier implementation omitted the partner draw and drifted towards one half even at zero taste. With the corrected rule, the change in the share over 14 years at zero taste has 95\% intervals that include zero at initial shares of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 (20 seeds each), and a taste near the calibrated value raises it by 2.3 percentage points [1.4, 3.1] from an initial share of 0.2: the market's own drive towards impact investing is small.

\subsection{Financing gains and the firm's decision rule}

Each firm has a fixed external financing exposure $E_i = 0.5\,P_{i,0}$, whose annual fee rate $r_{i,t} = r_f + s_{\text{fin}}\,E_i/(E_i + P_{i,t}^{\text{lag}}) - \lambda(z_{i,t} - \bar z_t)$ (with $r_f = 0.025$, $s_{\text{fin}} = 0.05$, and $\lambda = 0$ in the baseline contract; Section~\ref{sec:regimes} sweeps $\lambda > 0$) is repriced every $M = 60$ trading days. On each decision day $t_0$, firm~$i$ evaluates its local permanent price sensitivity $\partial\ln P_i/\partial s_i$ via a one-rebalance finite-difference perturbation ($\epsilon = 10^{-4}$) of its own signal across all 1,000 investors holding prices, wealth and expectations fixed, and maps it to its annual financing saving per SD of signal as a share of current market value:
\begin{equation}
B_i = \frac{E_i}{P_i}\left[\frac{s_{\text{fin}}\,E_i P_i}{(E_i + P_i)^2}\,\frac{\partial\ln P_i}{\partial s_i} + \lambda\right], \qquad \frac{\partial\ln P_i}{\partial s_i} = \max\!\left(\frac{\Delta Q_i}{\epsilon},\;0\right)\frac{\eta_{\text{perm}}}{\phi_0},
\label{eq:benefit}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta Q_i$ is aggregate incremental share demand from the perturbation, $\eta_{\text{perm}} = 0.486$ and $\phi_0 = 0.3$ (Appendix~\ref{app:formal}). Discounting daily at $r_d = 0.05$ over a five-year horizon ($H = 1{,}260$ trading days, $252$ per year), let $q(u) = \lfloor(t_0 + u)/M\rfloor M - 1$ be the quote day fixing day $t_0 + u$'s rate, let $t_r = t_0 + 120$ and $t_w = t_0 + 20$ be the due days of improvement and inflation, let $t_{a,1} = \lceil t_w / P_d \rceil P_d$ ($P_d = 60$) be the first audit on or after disclosure, and let $n_a(u) = \max\{0, \lfloor(q(u) - 1 - t_{a,1})/P_d\rfloor + 1\}$ count audits strictly before $q(u)$. Under uniform or zero decay without assurance, the three discount factors are
\begin{align}
A_r &= \frac{1}{252}\sum_{u=1}^{H} \mathbf{1}_{\{q(u)\ge t_r\}}\,e^{-r_d u/252}, \qquad
A_w = \frac{1}{252}\sum_{u=1}^{H} \mathbf{1}_{\{q(u)\ge t_w\}}\,(1 - q_i)^{n_a(u)}\,e^{-r_d u/252}, \label{eq:annuities}\\
D &= \frac{q_i\,e^{-r_d(t_{a,1} - t_0)/252}}{1 - (1 - q_i)\,e^{-r_d P_d/252}}, \label{eq:finefactor}
\end{align}
where $D$ sums the audit probability over all future audit dates because an undetected false report remains an open liability beyond the five-year financing horizon. Exact extensions for transitory mean reversion $\psi(d)$, displaced-inflation improvement $A_r^{\text{disp}}$, jump-targeted first-audit splits and pre-publication assurance checks $a_k$ are given in Appendix~\ref{app:formal}. With subsidy $\sigma$ per SD, proportional fine $p$ per SD of detected inflation, fixed fine $F$ and market value $P$, the interior values of the three actions are
\begin{align}
V_I &= \frac{(B A_r + \sigma)^2}{4c}, \label{eq:vi}\\
V_W &= \frac{\max\{B(1-\tau)A_w - pD,\, 0\}^2}{4wc} - \pi \frac{F}{P} D, \label{eq:vw}\\
V_M &= 0, \nonumber
\end{align}
with optimal sizes $(BA_r+\sigma)/2c$ and $(B(1-\tau)A_w - pD)/2wc$ before period and report-ceiling caps. The term $\pi$ is the probability that new inflation opens a new case (zero if the firm already carries undetected inflation under per-case booking; one under per-report booking). The firm selects the highest-value action (maintaining if $\max(V_I, V_W) \le 10^{-9}$ to avoid floating-point noise). The rule is myopic: firms re-optimise every period and do not anticipate their own future choices.

\subsection{The regulator and assurance interface}

In each 60-day period every firm is audited with probability 1\% in the baseline regime. An audit that finds inflation opens a case: the firm pays the fixed fine $F$ plus $p$ times its inflation, and the inflation is removed. Inflation added while earlier inflation is still undetected joins the same case and adds no fixed fine. We call this \emph{per-case booking}; the alternative, a separate fine for every false report, is tested in an exploratory supplement (Appendix~\ref{app:perreport}) and jointly with mean reversion (Appendix~\ref{app:joint}). In the reference setting $p = 0$ and $F$ equals CNY~10 million, the statutory maximum, which is $4.56\times10^{-5}$ of the average firm's initial value. Baseline transparency is $\tau = 0.1$. Section~\ref{sec:targeted} replaces uniform audits with public and hidden targeted rules (V, J) under the same expected audit budget, and Section~\ref{sec:assurance} introduces four reactive third-party assurance providers who face regulatory liability $L$ whenever an audit detects inflation in a report they inspected.

\subsection{Initialisation and calibration}

The market is initialised on the CSI~300 constituents of 9 October 2026, matched 300 of 300 on Huazheng composite ESG scores, half-year book equity and market value (Table~\ref{tab:parameters}). Scores are mapped to the latent standard-normal level by their empirical quantiles, so the scale saturates at a perfect score, and the latent level is linked to log value by a Gaussian copula with $\rho = 0.177$, converted from the sample Spearman correlation of 0.169. The dispersion of log intrinsic value, 0.915, is the cross-sectional SD of log market value.

One parameter was calibrated to a target. We set the median taste $\bar\theta = 0.0724$ so that, before any policy, the cross-sectional slope of dividend yield on the signal is about $-28$ basis points per SD (validation median $-28.25$). The target combines the estimate of \citet{zhao2024esg}, 0.19 percentage points of the cost of equity per Huazheng rating notch, with the 1.45 notches per SD in our sample; it is a cross-sectional association, not a causal effect. In our own sample, with Huazheng scores of October 2022--2024 and the implied cost of equity of \citet{easton2004pe} eight months later, the association is 14.5 basis points per SD [3.0, 26.0] (Appendix~\ref{app:investors}), so the target lies just above that interval. We kept the calibration, which was fixed before the experiments. The audit probability of 1\% per period is an assumption. It implies a discounted value of about 0.455 for paying one unit on detection; this is a present-value factor, not a detection rate, and is not comparable with the estimate of \citet{dyck2024pervasive} that about a third of corporate fraud is detected, which we cite only as evidence that detection is far from certain. The remaining firm-layer parameters --- natural decay, the washing cost ratio, the cost function, delays and the financing contract --- are design assumptions whose phase boundaries are mapped across Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:regimes}.

\section{Single-agent analytical benchmark and its limits}\label{sec:benchmark}
%======================================================================

To delineate clearly what follows from single-agent optimisation and what requires multi-agent simulation, we first characterise the firm's static decision rule as the inspectee's best response in an inspection game \citep{avenhaus2002inspection} holding market variables fixed. Two propositions summarise equations~\eqref{eq:vi}--\eqref{eq:vw} for an interior solution.

\begin{proposition}[Inflating under a fixed fine]\label{prop:fixed}
Let $\sigma = 0$, $p = 0$, $\pi = 1$ and $\tau < \tau^*$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold}). A firm inflates if and only if
\[
B^2\Big[\frac{(1-\tau)^2 A_w^2}{w} - A_r^2\Big] > 4\, c\, \frac{F}{P}\, D,
\]
that is, if and only if its market value exceeds $P^* = 4cFD \big/ \big(B^2[(1-\tau)^2A_w^2/w - A_r^2]\big)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proofsketch}
With $B>0$, $V_I>0$, so inflating must beat improving: $B^2(1-\tau)^2A_w^2/4wc - FD/P > B^2A_r^2/4c$.
\end{proofsketch}

Three consequences follow. The fixed fine decides \emph{whether} a firm inflates, not \emph{how much}, unlike a proportional fine, which lowers the net gain per SD. Because $F/P$ falls with size while $B$ is roughly size-independent and large firms improve more cheaply, large firms cross $P^*$ first; under a proportional fine the condition becomes $B[(1-\tau)A_w - \sqrt{w}A_r] > pD$, in which size enters only through $B$. And for a firm with an open, undetected case ($\pi = 0$) the condition reduces to $\tau < \tau^*$ and no longer depends on $F$: a higher fine can stop firms from starting to inflate but not from continuing. At an audit probability of 1\% per period, an undetected case lasts about 100 periods on average.

\begin{proposition}[Transparency threshold]\label{prop:threshold}
Let $\sigma = 0$ and $p = 0$. Firm $i$ inflates if and only if $\tau < \tau_i^*$, where
\[
\tau_i^* = 1 - \frac{\sqrt{w}}{A_w}\sqrt{A_r^2 + \frac{4\, c_i\, \pi_i\, (F/P_i)\, D}{B_i^2}}\,,
\]
and $\tau_i^* \le \tau^* \equiv 1 - \sqrt{w}\,A_r/A_w$, with equality if and only if $\pi_i F = 0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proofsketch}
Solve the inequality of Proposition~\ref{prop:fixed} for $1-\tau$.
\end{proofsketch}

Without a fine, every firm has the same threshold $\tau^*$, which depends only on $w$ and the timing that determines $A_w/A_r$, not on costs, size or financing gains. With the default timing and lasting improvement, $A_w/A_r \approx 1.03$, so $\tau^*(0.2) \approx 0.57$; when true performance reverts to the mean instead, $A_r$ falls sharply and $\tau^*(0.2)$ rises to $0.77$--$0.83$, generating the persistence phase boundary analysed in Section~\ref{sec:regimes}. A fixed fine spreads individual thresholds below $\tau^*$, while firms that already carry an open case keep $\tau^*$.

\paragraph{What the single-agent benchmark cannot answer.}
Propositions~\ref{prop:fixed}--\ref{prop:threshold} treat the financing gain $B_i$, market value $P_i$, audit probability $q_i$ and assurance quality $a_k$ as exogenous constants and ignore all cross-agent coupling. Consequently, while the propositions explain the baseline ordering of instruments and the transparency threshold check (confirming $\tau^*(w)$ in Section~\ref{sec:threshold} verifies implementation under feedback rather than providing independent theory), they cannot answer four fundamentally multi-agent questions addressed by our simulation experiments:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item How market-clearing prices and evolutionary investor dynamics jointly determine the level and channel composition of policy feedback (Section~\ref{sec:feedback});
\item Whether account-level model-free reinforcement learners can discover these thresholds inside realistic financial return noise (Section~\ref{sec:learning});
\item How fixed-budget targeted audit rules reallocate compliance across heterogeneous firm tiers and induce strategic threshold evasion once public (Section~\ref{sec:targeted}); and
\item How strategic third-party assurance providers co-evolve with client firms to produce escape cycles, quality oscillation cycles and non-monotonic liability returns (Section~\ref{sec:assurance}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Pre-registered experimental methodology}\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} To curb researcher degrees of freedom in complex multi-agent simulations \citep{olken2015promises}, every confirmatory study was specified in a version-controlled pre-registration protocol before implementation and execution. For Study~1 (Policy Instruments), the experimental design, primary hypotheses and classification thresholds were committed prior to the confirmatory run; a development run on separate seeds verified only pipeline integrity (completion, a null control with no actions, bit-identical pre-switch histories and metric windows). The confirmatory configuration was then frozen and executed on 30 fresh paired seeds (41001--41030) that appeared in none of the 6,039 configuration and archive files from earlier development work. Every subsequent confirmatory study---Study~2 (Targeted Audits, Section~\ref{sec:targeted}), Study~3 (Third-Party Assurance, Section~\ref{sec:assurance}), the Learning-Firm Redo (Section~\ref{sec:learning}), the Joint Robustness Study (Appendix~\ref{app:joint}), and the empirical Persistence, Financing-Channel and Investor-Response Studies (Section~\ref{sec:regimes} and Appendix~\ref{app:robust})---followed the same freeze-before-run protocol on disjoint seed blocks. Complete pre-registration commits, frozen configuration hashes, experiment identifiers, seed ranges and archive paths are consolidated in Appendix~\ref{app:repro} (Table~\ref{tab:provenance}).

\paragraph{Outcomes and questions.} The primary metric is the change in the equal-weighted mean true level from day 1200 to day 3500, in initial SD. Four primary questions were tested two-sided with the \citet{holm1979simple} correction:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item[P1] market preference: B minus market-off B, over the full period (days 0--3500);
\item[P2] instrument comparison: I minus F;
\item[P3] interaction: (IF $-$ B) $-$ (market-off IF $-$ market-off B);
\item[P4] size mechanism: B minus equal-cost B, in the large-minus-small difference of the change.
\end{itemize}
The other 36 contrasts are reported with unadjusted intervals and make no confirmatory claim. Because all scenarios in a market setting share their history up to the switch, contrasts are paired by seed, and intervals are paired 95\% $t$ intervals over the 30 seeds.

\paragraph{Monte Carlo inference and interpretation of $p$-values.}
In simulation experiments, increasing the number of paired seeds can render arbitrarily small differences---including magnitudes such as $0.003$~SD---statistically distinguishable from zero at extreme $p$-values ($10^{-12}$ to $10^{-30}$). Throughout this paper, paired 95\% $t$ intervals and Holm-adjusted $p$-values quantify \emph{only Monte Carlo random-seed variance} conditional on fixed model parameters and structural equations; they do \emph{not} capture parameter uncertainty or structural misspecification, which we evaluate separately through multi-regime boundary analysis (Section~\ref{sec:regimes} and Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). Accordingly, we report extreme simulation $p$-values concisely as $< 10^{-6}$ (with exact values preserved in the archived JSON reports) and treat formal hypothesis tests strictly as a directional noise-separation gate rather than a measure of substantive or empirical magnitude.

\paragraph{Phase classification.} A world is classified by its terminal-year change in the true level net of the decay it would have had without action ($G$) and its terminal-year inflation ($W$), with threshold 0.25~SD: real improvement ($G \ge 0.25$, $W < 0.25$), greenwashing trap ($G < 0.25$, $W \ge 0.25$), low improvement and low inflation, or mixed. A cell is labelled with a state only if at least 80\% of its seeds share it. All nine combinations of thresholds in $\{0.125, 0.25, 0.5\}$ are in the archive.

\paragraph{Failures, price-guard triggers and exploratory supplements.} Every run is kept in the archive. Of 1,770 runs in Study~1, all completed with zero divergence aborts ($|\ln(P/V)| > 5.0$); 37 runs triggered the daily log-return price guard ($|\Delta^{\mathrm{raw}}_{i,t}| > 0.3$; Section~\ref{sec:market}), clustered on six seeds (22 on seed~41016 alone, including 18 pre-switch triggers across high-taste phase-diagram cells and the size--ESG independence control, and 19 post-switch triggers in scenarios with $\tau \ge 0.5$). Guarded runs remain in all reported statistics; excluding them leaves P2 at $+0.207$ and P3 at $+0.333$. Following Study~1, we ran five exploratory supplements, each committed before execution on the same seeds (carrying no confirmatory weight): three intermediate decay rates (360 runs); a counterfactual replay separating market feedback (360 reruns and replays); the same decomposition in stronger markets (270 runs); a two-channel feedback split freezing investor evolution (240 runs); and three structural stress tests---mean-reverting true levels, per-report fines and firm-specific washing costs (600 runs). New options default to the original behaviour and reproduce earlier archives bit for bit.

\section{Core enforcement gaps, market feedback and the MARL learning barrier}\label{sec:results}
%======================================================================

This section reports Study~1 (Policy Instruments) together with its market-feedback decomposition and reinforcement-learning diagnosis. All results in Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:assurance} are reported in the high-headroom uniform-decay reference regime ($\delta = 0.2$~SD/year), where firms' scores slide without effort and enforcement gaps are clearly separated. Because our own panel-persistence test strongly rejects uniform decay ($T = 2{,}573, p < 10^{-6}$), absolute levels in this regime are not interpretable (even B declines by $-1.196$~SD and market-off worlds by $-1.825$~SD, exact accumulated decay); only between-scenario contrasts carry meaning, and Section~\ref{sec:regimes} maps the full phase boundaries across no decay, mean reversion, permanent-plus-transitory dynamics $Q$, and direct rating-linked financing.

\subsection{Investor preference is necessary under price-mediated financing}\label{sec:preference}

\subsection{Counterfactual market-feedback decomposition}\label{sec:feedback}

A rigorous test of what an artificial stock market contributes beyond single-agent optimisation is to sever market feedback exactly. Because each firm's decision depends on investors and other firms solely through its financing gain $B_i$ and market value $P_i$---and audit draws follow a separate scenario-shared stream---replaying the firm layer on its own saved $(B, P)$ paths reproduces full-market simulations bit for bit (360 of 360 runs). Replaying a policy scenario on the \emph{baseline} run's $(B, P)$ paths (\texttt{Rbase}) isolates the pure single-agent policy effect without market feedback; subtracting \texttt{Rbase} from the full model measures total endogenous market feedback. Furthermore, freezing each investor's type and preference at every 60-day imitation step to match the paired baseline run (\texttt{Frozen}) decomposes total feedback identically per seed into two distinct multi-agent channels: \emph{investor evolution} ($E = \text{Full} - \text{Frozen}$) and \emph{price--demand reallocation} ($D = \text{Frozen} - \text{Rbase}$), with $E + D \equiv \text{Feedback}$.

Table~\ref{tab:feedback} reveals two methodological findings that justify modelling the coupled market explicitly even when the qualitative ordering I $>$ F $>$ B holds in 30 of 30 seeds with or without feedback:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Opposing-channel cancellation.} Judging solely from net feedback on I~$-$~B ($-0.008$~SD [$-0.022$, $+0.007$]), one might mistakenly conclude that market feedback is inert under transparency. The channel split proves otherwise: stopping greenwashing under $\tau = 0.6$ narrows return dispersion tilted toward impact investors, driving a $-1.96$~pp outflow of impact investors by day 3500 that weakens transparency by $E = -0.040$~SD ($-12.1\%$ of the full effect; $-20.9\%$ on I~$-$~F). \item \emph{Static-snapshot distortion.} Even before feedback, the market dynamically determines the trajectory of $(B_i, P_i)$. Replaying the firm decision layer on a single static snapshot taken at day 1200 (as in the online sandbox) preserves qualitative directions but overstates policy magnitudes by 27--71\%.
\end{itemize}
Thus, while the qualitative baseline ordering of Study~1 follows from the single-firm rule (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}), exact counterfactual replay exposes hidden evolutionary-versus-price channel cancellation and demonstrates that genuinely multi-agent market dynamics govern both quantitative magnitudes and the strategic couplings examined next in Sections~\ref{sec:targeted} and~\ref{sec:assurance}.

\subsection{Account-level model-free reinforcement learning: a signal-to-noise barrier}\label{sec:learning}

A central question in multi-agent systems is how boundedly rational, learning agents behave relative to forward-looking optimisers \citep{shoham2007multiagent,busoniu2008comprehensive,hernandezleal2019survey}. We tested replacing the NPV decision rule with tabular Q-learning \citep{watkins1992qlearning} trained directly on firms' realised 60-day accounts (financing-fee changes minus resource costs and fines, scaled by initial value). Even after reducing the action space to seven discrete options (three magnitudes each of improvement and inflation plus maintaining), applying polynomial learning rates \citep{evendar2003learning}, conditioning states on the firm's signal tercile and scaling training worlds to full market size, learners failed the pre-set state--action coverage gate in the post-policy large-firm group. Worse, two identically configured tables trained on independent seeds inflated in 69\% and 15\% of decisions, making policy comparisons indistinguishable from training noise.

\paragraph{Quantifying the signal-to-noise barrier.}
A diagnostic experiment with purely random exploration ($\varepsilon = 1$, 60,800 decisions across 8 full-scale worlds) identifies a fundamental structural barrier rather than a tuning defect, echoing MARL analyses of reward signal-to-noise ratios in stochastic multi-agent domains \citep{agogino2008analyzing}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Extreme SNR mismatch.} At the calibrated ESG premium, the median NPV gain of the best action over maintaining is just \textbf{0.07 basis points} of initial market value (0.52~bp at the 90th percentile), and the expected statutory fine per period is $\sim 0.005$~bp. Meanwhile, within a given state, the 20-period discounted reward---the horizon over which financing savings arrive---has a standard deviation of \textbf{190 basis points}, and even single-period maintaining rewards net of common shocks carry 2.1~bp of residual noise. As an order-of-magnitude benchmark, separating a 0.07~bp signal from 190~bp noise requires roughly $1.2\times10^{8}$ samples per state--action cell ($2\times10^{6}$ at the 90th percentile), compared to $10^3$--$10^4$ visits achievable in full-market training archives.
\item \emph{Delayed credit assignment and non-stationarity.} Immediate action costs ($-1.9$ to $-60.9$~bp) are clearly visible, so learners quickly learn cost avoidance, whereas financing payoffs from real improvement arrive only after a 120-day implementation lag and subsequent repricing inside a non-stationary market of 200 adapting firms and 1,000 evolving investors. Without tracking in-progress improvements in the state, one-step TD updates misattribute delayed gains to whatever action occurs when they arrive.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pre-registered redo: an undetectable greenwashing trap.}
To test whether delayed credit assignment biases model-free learners toward faster-paying inflation, we ran a pre-registered 540-job Learning-Firm Redo (full provenance in Table~\ref{tab:provenance}, Appendix~\ref{app:repro}) training ten independent replicates per scenario under two state encodings: version~A (baseline state) and version~V (replacing the cost tier with cumulative real improvement including in-progress work), evaluated on five validation seeds against discrete-grid NPV firms across five Holm-corrected primary questions.

Coverage remained unmet (91 of 100 tables passed; 8 of 9 failures occurred in post-policy large-firm groups, reflecting the structural scarcity of 20 large firms per world), and training seed randomness dominated (between-table SD of greenwashing share was 14--18 times within-table SD; ICC $\approx 0.99$). One primary hypothesis was supported: under transparency I, version-A learners inflated in 23.7\% of decisions versus 3.8\% for discrete NPV ($+19.9$~pp [$+7.2$, $+32.6$], Holm $p = 0.031$). Roughly two thirds of those inflations (15.5\% of all decisions under I) occurred where discrete NPV would not inflate and would almost always improve. Crucially, however, their median loss against the NPV optimum was just \textbf{0.042 basis points}---fifty times smaller than single-period reward noise. Learners did not ``learn'' that greenwashing pays; they remained trapped in arbitrary small-scale inflation because the loss was statistically invisible. All other four primary questions were unresolved: making improvement visible in version~V reduced trap decisions under I from 15.5\% to 9.3\% (Holm $p = 0.47$) and barely moved baseline true change ($+0.003$~SD; on scratch pre-policy tables V cut washing by $-34.7$~pp, shifting actions to maintaining rather than improvement); learners' true-ESG response to transparency was $+0.157$~SD [$+0.040$, $+0.274$] (Holm $p = 0.057$), and their response to the quadrupled fine was $+0.001$~SD (confirming that 1\% low-probability penalties are invisible to account-level RL). Dropping the five replicates containing a coverage-failing table left all five questions unresolved.

\paragraph{Methodological implications for financial MAS.}
Rather than a mere implementation setback, this diagnosis establishes a substantive boundary condition for agent design in artificial financial markets: pure ex-post, model-free trial-and-error on firm financial statements is informationally infeasible at realistic ESG financing premia. Real corporations do not discover disclosure policies by blind tabular Q-learning on noisy quarterly earnings; they combine forward-looking financial appraisal (captured by our NPV benchmark) with external market signals and peer benchmarking. Methodologically, our negative result applies specifically to unmodified account-level tabular Q-learning: rigorously modelling boundedly rational firms in low-SNR financial MAS will require variance-reduced estimators---such as control variates and baseline subtraction \citep{sutton2018reinforcement} or difference rewards \citep{agogino2008analyzing}---contextual bandits conditioned on local valuation estimates, or inter-firm social imitation analogous to our investors' pairwise \citet{schlag1998imitate} rule. Because unmodified Q-learning fails coverage and is dominated by training variance, boundedly rational firms remain outside our main institutional comparisons.

\section{Strategic audit allocation: evasion and cross-tier displacement}\label{sec:targeted}
%======================================================================

Having analysed baseline enforcement gaps and market feedback, we now turn to the first of two explicitly coupled strategic enforcement layers: \emph{regulator--firm interaction under rule-based audit allocation} (Study~2: Targeted Audits). In Study~1, every firm faced a uniform 1\% audit probability per period, leaving large firms inflating in 66\% of decisions and open cases continuing regardless of the fixed fine. Unlike a fixed fine, the audit probability $q_i$ governs both inflation persistence ($A_w$) and the detection discount ($D$), reaching open cases as well as new ones. In the spirit of Stackelberg Security Games and Audit Games \citep{conitzer2006computing,kiekintveld2009computing,tambe2011security,blocki2015audit,sinha2018stackelberg}, we ask what happens when a regulator reallocates a fixed expected audit budget across heterogeneous firms using two canonical public heuristics commonly proposed in supervisory practice---proportional targeting by firm market value and anomaly targeting by single-period score jumps---and isolate strategic commitment effects by contrasting public against hidden rules.

\paragraph{Design and formal allocation rules.} Study~2 was pre-registered prior to implementation and executed on 30 fresh paired seeds (42001--42030, 450 runs; full provenance in Table~\ref{tab:provenance}, Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). On each audit date, firm~$i$ is audited if a scenario-shared uniform draw falls below $q_i \in [0,1]$, subject to the identical expected budget $K = N\bar q = 2$ (matching uniform $\bar q = 1\%$ across $N = 200$ firms):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Random} (B): $q_i = \bar q = 0.01$ for all $i$.
\item \emph{By value} (V): proportional to current market capitalisation $P_i$ with iterative unit-cap renormalisation. Initialising the capped set $S = \emptyset$, the regulator sets
\begin{equation}
q_i = \frac{(K - |S|)\,P_i}{\sum_{j \notin S} P_j} \quad \text{for } i \notin S,
\label{eq:audit_v}
\end{equation}
clamps any $q_i > 1$ to $1$ and moves those firms into $S$, repeating until $q_i \in [0,1]$ for all $i$ (exact budget conservation $\sum_i q_i = K$). Initially, median audit rates are 0.41\% for small firms, 3.0\% for large firms and 8.0\% for the largest firm.
\item \emph{By jump} (J): firm~$i$ is placed in the flagged set $F$ if its pre-discount reported score $\min(g_i + x_i, C)$ increased by more than $h = 0.125$~SD since the previous audit date ($r_i^{\mathrm{prev}}$). If $m = |F| = 0$, probabilities revert to $q_i = \bar q$; otherwise half the budget is uniform and half splits equally across flagged firms,
\begin{equation}
q_i = 0.5\,\bar q + \mathbf{1}_{\{i \in F\}}\,\frac{0.5\,K}{m},
\label{eq:audit_j}
\end{equation}
with excess-probability renormalisation: whenever $0 < m < N$ and flagged probabilities exceed $1$, flagged firms are clamped to $1$ and the surplus $\sum_{j \in F}(q_j - 1)/(N - m)$ is distributed equally across unflagged firms so $\sum_i q_i = K$.
\item \emph{Doubled random audits} (A2): $q_i = 2\bar q = 0.02$, twice the budget, as a capacity benchmark.
\end{itemize}
Under public rules (V, J), firms best-respond to their induced audit schedule: under V using their current $q_i$; under J by forecasting the next flagged count as $\max(1, m_t)$, computing their sub-threshold inflation headroom $x_i^{\mathrm{safe}} = h - \Delta_i^{\mathrm{pending}} + \delta P_d / 252$ (with the analogous transitory-reversion term given in Appendix~\ref{app:formal}), and choosing the higher-NPV option between capping inflation at $x_i^{\mathrm{safe}}$ (preserving first-audit rate $q_{i,1} = 0.5\bar q$) and accepting the flagged first-audit rate $q_{i,1} = \min(1, 0.5\bar q + 0.5K/\max(1, m_t))$ (ignoring any flag effect on older undetected inflation, which conservatively understates evasion). Under hidden rules (\Vh, \Jh), firms act as under uniform 1\% audits so targeting alters detection only. The 15 scenarios comprise B, I, F, A2, V, \Vh, J, \Jh{} and VF (value targeting plus $4\times$ fixed fine) in the reference regime ($\delta = 0.2$), plus six scenarios without decay. Five Holm-corrected primary questions (Table~\ref{tab:audit_primary}) and five analytical predictions were pre-registered; all five predictions held. All 450 runs completed with zero divergence aborts; five runs on seed~42030 triggered the daily return clamp (none in primary contrasts).

At an equal expected audit budget, both public targeting rules perform significantly \emph{worse} than uniform random audits on equal-weighted true ESG (E1: $-0.191$~SD; E2: $-0.136$~SD), even though both catch far more misconduct per inspection: audit hit rates jump from 28\% under B to 50\% under V and 69\% under J (Figure~\ref{fig:targeted}b; Table~\ref{tab:audit_describe}). Crucially, when the exact same allocation rules are kept hidden, neither differs significantly from random audits (\Vh{}~$-$~B $= -0.005$~SD [$-0.012$, $+0.001$]; \Jh{}~$-$~B $= -0.005$~SD [$-0.011$, $+0.001$]). Publishing the jump rule alone costs $-0.131$~SD (E3), isolating strategic adversary response as the dominant failure mechanism.

\paragraph{Cross-tier displacement versus relative concentration.}
Value targeting pushes greenwashing from large to small firms---and does so via a genuine \emph{absolute expansion} in small-firm violations, not merely a shrinking denominator (Figure~\ref{fig:targeted}a and Table~\ref{tab:displacement}). Large firms' inflating rate drops from 64.4\% to 46.6\% (489.3 to 354.5 decisions per world), while small firms' rate surges sevenfold from 4.8\% to 35.2\% (217.8 to 1,606.5 decisions per world); small firms' share of all inflating decisions jumps from 14.1\% to 56.8\% (and from 2.7\% to 30.1\% without decay). The same sharp statistical distinction holds in the Persistence Study under estimated dynamics $Q$ (Table~\ref{tab:displacement}; Appendix~\ref{app:persistence}): V~$-$~B raises small firms' share by $+24.3$~pp (14.1\% to 38.4\%) while more than tripling their absolute inflating count (270.9 to 896.0 per world; within-tier rate 5.9\% to 19.7\%), whereas raising fixed fines (F~$-$~B) increases large firms' share by $+3.7$~pp via \emph{relative concentration} as large firms' absolute count actually falls (557.6 to 488.2), simply less steeply than smaller tiers. Under V, the size divide flips from $-0.30$ to $+0.32$~SD (E4) and value-weighted true ESG rises ($+0.053$~SD [$+0.043$, $+0.063$]), but equal-weighted true ESG falls because small firms represent 60\% of firms and lose far more deterrence than large firms gain: the detection discount $D(q)$ is strongly concave ($0.455$ at 1\% vs.\ $0.87$ at 8\%), lowering the median small firm's normalised expected fixed fine from 2.46 to 1.37 while raising the median large firm's only from 0.34 to 0.53. Nor does V close large firms' open cases: Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} requires $q_i \ge 10.5\%$ per period to halt continuing inflation at $\tau = 0.1$; all 81,253 continuing inflations under V occurred below that rate, and all 313 firm-periods reaching it had zero.

\paragraph{Threshold evasion under jump targeting.}
When the jump rule is public, firms strategically compress inflation below the threshold $h = 0.125$~SD: the fraction of new inflations flagged at the next audit date drops from 54\% under \Jh{} to 44\% under J ($-9.6$~pp [$-10.9$, $-8.2$]; $-13.8$~pp without decay). Unflagged firms then enjoy a halved 0.5\% audit rate, emboldening previously deterred small firms to open new cases (raising small-firm inflating rates by $+8.7$~pp over \Jh{}, from 5.2\% to 13.9\%, and their share of all inflating decisions from 14.6\% to 25.6\%).

\paragraph{Capacity versus reallocation.}
Doubling random audits (A2) lifts true ESG by $+0.118$~SD [$+0.103$, $+0.133$] and reduces inflation across all three tiers (though large firms remain at 54\%), beating both targeted rules decisively (V~$-$~A2 $= -0.309$~SD; J~$-$~A2 $= -0.254$~SD). Transparency still beats combining value targeting with a quadrupled fixed fine (E5: I~$-$~VF $= +0.156$~SD; VF~$-$~F $= +0.034$~SD equal-weighted, $+0.181$~SD value-weighted). On these fresh seeds, Study~1's core comparison replicates cleanly (I~$-$~F $= +0.191$~SD [$+0.165$, $+0.216$]). Without natural decay, every sign holds at compressed magnitudes (V~$-$~B $= -0.003$, J~$-$~B $= -0.008$, I~$-$~VF $= +0.026$~SD).

\paragraph{Boundary relative to Stackelberg security games.}
An important scope boundary connects directly to the Stackelberg Security Games and Audit Games literature \citep{conitzer2006computing,kiekintveld2009computing,tambe2011security,blocki2015audit,sinha2018stackelberg}. We did \emph{not} compute a Stackelberg-optimal randomized audit schedule over our continuous-state firm population. Accordingly, Study~2 does not claim that no optimal targeted audit rule can outperform uniform random audits. Rather, it demonstrates the \emph{adversarial failure of two canonical, intuitive public heuristics} once inspectees observe them under a fixed budget: reallocating inspections toward large firms or sudden jumps thins deterrence on small firms and sub-threshold inflation, so maximizing detection hit rates backfires on system-wide compliance. Computing budget-constrained Stackelberg-optimal dynamic audit schedules---accounting for open-case persistence and firm size concavity---is a direct algorithmic follow-up. Under estimated persistence $Q$ (Section~\ref{sec:regimes} and Appendix~\ref{app:persistence}), the displacement share under V ($+24.3$~pp) and jump-rule evasion (J~$-$~\Jh{} $= -0.010$~SD) both persist, even as high transitory rating volatility triggers flagging on $\sim 74$ of 200 firms per date.

\section{Coupled firm--assurer co-evolution: escape cycles, quality cycles and non-monotonic liability}\label{sec:assurance}
%======================================================================

The paper's most pronounced emergent multi-agent dynamics arise when transparency is delegated to \emph{strategic third-party assurance providers} (Study~3: Third-Party Assurance). Unlike abstract transparency $\tau$, real-world assurance introduces a multi-agent governance conflict: firms pay providers and, absent mandatory assignment, choose among them \citep{duflo2013truth,skreta2009ratings,bolton2012credit}. China's 2024 exchange guidelines do not mandate assurance but require disclosure of provider independence and assurance reports when hired \citep[Art.~58]{sse2024guideline}, while Article~213(3) of the Securities Law fines negligent securities service providers one to ten times business income, or CNY~0.5--5 million in the low-income band \citep[Art.~213(3)]{securitieslaw2019}. Taking CNY~5 million as the benchmark liability $L$ (without taking a legal stance on ESG applicability), we embed four adaptive assurance providers inside the unified market (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}) and examine how endogenous provider quality, liability doses and client choice interact. None of the three core emergent dynamics below---annual escape cycles, endogenous quality oscillation cycles and non-monotonic liability deterrence---can be derived from the static single-firm equations of Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}.

\paragraph{Design and formal decision rules.} Study~3 was pre-registered prior to implementation and run on 30 fresh paired seeds (43001--43030, 540 runs; provenance in Table~\ref{tab:provenance}, Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). Every 60-day period, each assured firm is inspected \emph{before publication} by its assigned or chosen provider $k \in \{1,\dots,4\}$. Provider~$k$ detects and strips all inflation with probability $a_k \in [0,1]$ (without fining or reporting the firm), and collects a per-period inspection fee $f_{\mathrm{assure}}$. At the start of each assurance year (four periods, 240 trading days), each provider updates quality via myopic cost-plus-liability minimisation,
\begin{equation}
a_k = \min\!\big(1,\; \lambda_L s_k\big), \qquad \lambda_L = \frac{L\bar q}{2\kappa},
\label{eq:assurer}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa a^2$ is effort cost, $\bar q = 1\%$ is the regulator's subsequent audit probability, $L$ is the provider's liability if the regulator later catches inflation in an inspected report, and $s_k$ is the fraction of provider~$k$'s inspections during the previous year that encountered an inflated report. Tested liability doses span $0, 1, 4, 10, 16$ and $100\times$ the CNY~5 million benchmark (interpreted relative to assumed $\kappa$). Providers are either randomly assigned (R) or chosen annually by firms (F), under mandatory universal assurance (ALL), large-firm-only mandates (LRG) or voluntary purchase (VOL16).

Let $V^*_i(a) = \max\{V_{I,i}(a),\, V_{W,i}(a),\, 0\}$ denote firm~$i$'s optimal single-period NPV (as a fraction of $P_i$) when facing pre-publication check probability $a$ (with closed-form assurance-adjusted annuity $A_w(a)$ and fine factor $D(a)$ given in Appendix~\ref{app:formal}). At the start of each assurance year:
\begin{equation}
k^*_i = \arg\max_{k \in \{1,\dots,4\}} V^*_i(a_k), \qquad
\begin{cases}
\text{switch to } k^*_i \text{ under F} & \iff V^*_i(a_{k^*_i}) > V^*_i(a_{k_{\mathrm{curr}}}), \\
\text{buy from } k^*_i \text{ under VOL16} & \iff V^*_i(a_{k^*_i}) - 4 f_{\mathrm{assure}}/P_i > V^*_i(0),
\end{cases}
\label{eq:assurer_choice}
\end{equation}
breaking ties toward the lowest provider index. Because pre-publication checks act every period against a 1\% regulatory audit rate, Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} implies that a modest quality of $a^* = 0.0827$ suffices to deter any interior-solution firm from inflating at baseline $\tau = 0.1$. Five Holm-corrected primary contrasts (Table~\ref{tab:assurance_primary}) and six pre-registered analytical predictions were tested; all six predictions held.

\paragraph{Price-guard triggers.} All 540 runs completed with zero divergence aborts ($|\ln(P/V)| > 5.0$); 29 runs (5.4\%) triggered the daily log-return clamp ($|\Delta^{\mathrm{raw}}_{i,t}| > 0.3$; Section~\ref{sec:market}). Because all 18 scenarios on a paired seed share bit-identical pre-switch histories, 18 of these 29 triggers originate from a single pre-switch tail return on seed~43007 (day~323, nearly four years before the day-1201 policy switch) replicated across every scenario; nine arise on seed~43026 (day~1916); and two are isolated post-switch hits (seed~43004 \texttt{ALL\_R10}, day~2662; seed~43011 \texttt{ALL\_R16}, day~2590). All guarded runs remain in reported statistics; excluding them leaves all five primary verdicts unchanged (C1 $+0.257$, C2 $-0.186$, C3 $+0.043$, C4 $+0.549$, C5 $+0.027$~SD).

\paragraph{Emergent dynamic 1: client-switching escape cycles under firm choice.}
With strong liability ($16\times$) and random assignment, mandatory assurance is highly effective: ALL\_R16 raises true ESG by $+0.273$~SD over baseline (C1) and cuts inflating decisions from 19.0\% to 6.2\% across all tiers (Table~\ref{tab:assurance_describe}). Allowing firms to choose their own provider destroys nearly three quarters of that gain: ALL\_F16 lies $-0.199$~SD below ALL\_R16 (C2; Figure~\ref{fig:assurance}a) and pushes inflating decisions to 21.0\%---slightly \emph{above} no assurance (19.0\%; Figure~\ref{fig:assurance}b)---even though providers' unweighted mean quality is actually higher under firm choice (0.131 vs.\ 0.110). The gap between unweighted provider quality (0.131) and client-weighted quality faced by firms (0.110) reveals an emergent \emph{escape cycle}: whenever inflating firms cluster at a lax provider, that provider's prior-year contamination $s_k$ spikes, causing it to tighten $a_k$ the next year; at the start of the new year, roughly 46 firms switch en masse to a provider whose previously clean clients induced a low $a_k$. Across all 30 seeds, \textbf{96.9\%} of assured firms' inflating decisions (46,489 of 47,953) come from clients of the year's single lowest-quality provider (97.4\% without decay; 95.6\% under estimated persistence $Q$ in the Persistence Study). A Shapley decomposition on the Persistence Study archive (Table~\ref{tab:displacement}) confirms that this is a coupled client-flow and behavioural-amplification mechanism rather than mere mechanical relabelling: moving from ALL\_R16 to ALL\_F16 raises the laxest provider's client share from 27.7\% to 52.2\% ($+24.5$~pp), raises within-client inflating rates at the laxest provider from 11.0\% to 38.1\% ($+27.0$~pp), and drops inflating rates at stricter providers from 11.9\% to 1.9\% ($-10.0$~pp); Shapley attribution splits the $+69.8$~pp jump in the laxest provider's share of assured greenwashing into $+15.3$~pp [$+14.1$, $+16.6$] from client reallocation and $+54.4$~pp [$+52.3$, $+56.6$] from within-client behavioural amplification.

\paragraph{Emergent dynamics 2 and 3: quality oscillation cycles and non-monotonic liability.}
Even when firms cannot switch providers (assigned R), coupled annual best-response between firms and assurers prevents static convergence. Figure~\ref{fig:assurance}a--b reveals a striking \emph{non-monotonic dose response}: against baseline, assigned assurance yields $+0.000, +0.046, +0.145, +0.256, +0.273$ and $+0.232$~SD at $0\times, 1\times, 4\times, 10\times, 16\times$ and $100\times$ liability. Increasing liability more than sixfold from $16\times$ to $100\times$ raises mean provider quality nearly fourfold (0.110 to 0.425) yet \emph{worsens} true ESG ($+0.232$ vs.\ $+0.273$~SD) and \emph{increases} inflating decisions from 6.2\% to 8.6\%. The pre-registered quality-cycle trajectory in Figure~\ref{fig:assurance}c directly exposes this feedback loop: once high $a_k > 0.0827$ strips accumulated inflation stock, next year's observed contamination $s_k$ plunges, pulling next year's myopic quality $a_k$ back below the deterrence threshold and reigniting a wave of inflation. Under ALL\_R16, annual mean quality crosses above $0.0827$ and subsequently falls back below it in 28 of 30 seeds, with inflation recurring during the final four assurance years in all 30 seeds. At the low end ($1\times$, ALL\_R1, mean $a_k = 0.014$), weak assurance creates a subtle \emph{fine-insurance} effect predicted analytically: because pre-publication correction prevents regulatory fines on caught inflation, clean firms without open cases inflate $+0.9$~pp [$+0.7$, $+1.2$] more often and total inflating rises to 20.0\%, even though true ESG edges up $+0.046$~SD (C3) due to slightly larger average improvement steps (0.0229 vs.\ 0.0209~SD, post-hoc).

\paragraph{Partial mandates, voluntary assurance and transparency benchmark.}
Mandating assurance only for the 20 large firms (LRG\_R16) narrows the size divide ($+0.563$~SD, C4) by dropping large-firm inflating from 64.7\% to 24.1\%, but mid- and small-firm inflating rises (34.4\% $\to$ 38.8\% and 3.6\% $\to$ 6.1\%), leaving unassured firms responsible for 86.4\% of all inflating decisions (vs.\ 65.9\% in B) and limiting the equal-weighted true-ESG gain to $+0.057$~SD. Transparency ($\tau = 0.6$) still edges out the strongest tested assurance configuration (C5: I~$-$~ALL\_R16 $= +0.028$~SD; $+0.075$~SD value-weighted; $+0.227$~SD over ALL\_F16). No firm buys voluntary assurance (VOL16 is bit-identical to B) because investors observe signals only and do not reward assurance directly. Without natural decay, every contrast keeps its sign at smaller true-ESG magnitudes ($+0.030$ for C1, $-0.010$ for C2, $+0.004$ for C3, $+0.009$ for C5), while firm choice still generates more inflating decisions than no assurance (5.5\% vs.\ 4.9\% in B and 2.8\% in ALL\_R16, with $\sim 13$ firms switching per year).

\paragraph{Information-structure boundary and institutional scope.}
One multi-agent modelling assumption warrants explicit scrutiny. Equation~\eqref{eq:assurer} assumes that at each year-end, provider~$k$ knows the exact prior-year incidence of inflation $s_k$ among its clients. Because a provider detects inflation before publication only with probability $a_k$, assuming exact ex-post knowledge of $s_k$ is a strong \emph{lagged ex-post review / industry-audit revelation} approximation---as if delayed regulatory inspections, post-publication restatements or industry peer reviews reveal prior-year portfolio contamination with a one-year lag. In practice, with regulatory audits at 1\% per period, providers would observe only noisy, sparse signals of missed inflation; noisy Bayesian updating from detected failures alone would slow quality adjustments and could alter the amplitude or period of both quality cycles and escape cycles. Similarly, providers are myopic (ignoring anticipated client departures), and the model omits provider reputation, exit, mandatory rotation, regulator reporting, minimum assurance floors and public disclosure of assurance status (the latter limiting the external validity of VOL16). Finally, Appendix~\ref{app:repro} documents a post-experiment code audit that separated firm assurance fees (transfers) from provider effort costs in cost ledgers and moved pre-publication draws onto an independent calendar-day stream; this paper reports the preserved original runs, as none of the reported contrasts use cost-efficiency ratios and all five primary contrasts compare either one assured scenario against an unassured baseline or two scenarios with identical 200-firm scope and start date.

\paragraph{Distinguishing three statistical mechanisms of ``greenwashing displacement''.}
Because shares of total inflating decisions can rise either through a shrinking denominator or through genuine behavioural expansion, Table~\ref{tab:displacement} brings together all three enforcement-gap channels reported in Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:assurance} alongside within-group rates, absolute per-world decision counts and Shapley attribution. Only by reporting absolute counts and within-group rates beside shares can we cleanly separate \emph{relative concentration} (under F~$-$~B, large firms' share rises because small-firm inflation falls faster while large firms' absolute count declines), \emph{absolute expansion} (under V~$-$~B, small firms' absolute inflating count increases sevenfold at $\delta = 0.2$ and triples under $Q$), and \emph{client reallocation plus within-client behavioural amplification} (under ALL\_F16~$-$~ALL\_R16, total assured inflating increases sharply while Shapley attribution shows 78\% of the $+69.8$~pp concentration at the laxest provider arises from behavioural amplification within client pools rather than client switching alone).

\section{Phase boundaries, empirical scope and model limitations}\label{sec:regimes}
%======================================================================

Why did we frame this study as a \emph{mechanism and institutional design study} rather than an empirical policy forecast? Across the 20 three-policy comparisons and replications compiled in Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:robust}, the fixed-dose ordering I $>$ F $>$ B holds on equal-weighted true ESG, and the targeted-audit and assurance displacement directions persist across the tested persistence and financing regimes (Table~\ref{tab:regimes}). Yet our own pre-registered empirical checks reveal two sharp \emph{structural phase boundaries}---governed by (i)~how fast true ESG performance reverts to the mean, and (ii)~how strongly financing rates price ESG signals---across which true-ESG magnitudes and displacement shares undergo order-of-magnitude regime shifts. Putting these boundaries in the main text clarifies exactly what the model robustly establishes and where quantitative extrapolation stops.

\subsection{Phase boundary I: persistence of true ESG dynamics}\label{sec:decay}

\paragraph{Uniform decay and its empirical rejection.}
Within the uniform-decay family, natural decay creates headroom for inflation: a firm that does nothing watches its score slide and can replace it cheaply by inflating. Figure~\ref{fig:decay} maps the five tested uniform decay rates ($\delta \in [0, 0.2]$~SD/year, combining the two pre-registered endpoints with the three exploratory intermediate rates). Raising $\delta$ from 0 to 0.2 lifts baseline inflating decisions from 4.7\% to 22.3\%, I~$-$~B from $+0.040$ to $+0.331$~SD, F~$-$~B from $+0.005$ to $+0.125$~SD, and I~$-$~F from $+0.035$ to $+0.205$~SD, with strongly convex growth (at $\delta = 0.1$, I~$-$~B is 39\% of its $\delta = 0.2$ value).

However, empirical rating dynamics do not follow uniform decay. In our 2009--2025 quarterly panel of Huazheng ESG scores (225,754 firm-dates across 5,816 A-share firms; secondary-source panel spot-checked on October 2025 against an official-website snapshot, with historical waves unverified against an official export; Appendix~\ref{app:reversion}), ratings exhibit no downward trend within the longest single-methodology regime R1 (January 2009--January 2021) and revert toward the cross-sectional mean. Formal simulated minimum-distance estimation matching R1's rank-normal score autocorrelations at lags 1, 2, 4, 8 and 12 quarters (Persistence Study; Appendix~\ref{app:persistence}) strongly \emph{rejects uniform decay} ($T = 2{,}573, p < 10^{-6}$, hitting the upper grid bound $\hat\delta = 0.30$ while leaving lag-12 correlation at $0.774$ vs.\ $0.548$ in the data). Pure mean reversion $R(\kappa)$ ($T = 130$) and a permanent-plus-transitory decomposition $Q(\kappa_T, s_P)$ ($T = 38.7$ on 30 seeds, $\hat\kappa_T = 0.505$, $\hat s_P = 0.423$) are also rejected at $p < 10^{-6}$, and $Q$ fails pre-registered parameter recovery (4--9\% upward bias in $\hat\kappa_T$), leaving $Q$ as the closest-fitting rejected benchmark rather than an empirically validated process.

\paragraph{The $\tau^* = 0.6$ persistence phase transition.}
Figure~\ref{fig:regimes}a exposes the exact mechanism driving the magnitude collapse across persistence regimes. When deliberate real improvement lasts permanently---under uniform decay, no decay, or the permanent-improvement variant of $Q$ where deliberate effort enters the permanent anchor $\mu_i$---the improvement annuity $A_r = 3.73$~years keeps $\tau^*(0.2) = 0.57 < 0.6$, so transparency $\tau = 0.6$ halts inflation entirely and yields substantial true-ESG advantages over the quadrupled fine ($+0.205$~SD under $\delta = 0.2$, $+0.077$~SD under $Q$ with permanent improvement, $+0.035$~SD under $\delta = 0$). By contrast, when deliberate improvement reverts like transitory shocks---under exploratory pure mean reversion ($\kappa \in \{0.32, 0.50\}$; Appendix~\ref{app:reversion}), joint reversion plus per-report fines (Appendix~\ref{app:joint}), or the main transitory-improvement block of the Persistence Study under $Q$ ($\kappa_T = 0.505, s_P = 0.423$; Appendix~\ref{app:persistence})---$A_r$ falls from $3.73$ to $1.50$--$2.01$~years while undetected inflation still survives until an audit. Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} then pushes $\tau^*(0.2)$ to $0.77$--$0.83 > 0.6$: transparency $\tau = 0.6$ no longer stops inflation, and I~$-$~F shrinks to $+0.003$--$0.012$~SD. Indeed, in the main transitory block under $Q$, all eight Holm-corrected primary directional contrasts remain strongly supported (Table~\ref{tab:stage_h3}), yet the descriptive prediction that I produces fewer inflating decisions than F fails to separate them (18.35\% vs.\ 18.33\%, difference $+0.02$~pp [$-0.06$, $+0.10$]).

Crucially, however, \emph{compliance-arbitrage displacement shares do not collapse across this persistence boundary} (Table~\ref{tab:regimes}): under $Q$ in the Persistence Study, value targeting still raises small firms' share of inflating decisions by $+24.3$~pp (tripling their absolute inflating count), firm choice still sends 95.6\% of assured greenwashing to the year's laxest provider ($+69.8$~pp over assignment), and publishing the jump rule still degrades true ESG ($-0.010$~SD). Because the rating panel cannot identify whether deliberate ESG investments fade like transitory shocks or persist permanently, both the transitory ($+0.003$~SD) and permanent-improvement ($+0.077$~SD) $Q$ regimes must be read side by side.

\subsection{Phase boundary II: financing-incentive intensity and saturation}

The second phase boundary concerns the strength of the financing incentive firms act upon. In the price-mediated contract of Studies~1--3 and the Persistence Study, calibrated investor taste generates a local financing-rate slope of only $\sim 2.2$~bp per SD of public signal. Yet in our pre-registered A-share panel regression (41,662 firm-years, 2009--2024; Financing-Channel Study, Appendix~\ref{app:financing}), a one-SD higher Huazheng score is associated with a debt cost 28.0~bp lower next year [23.4, 32.5] (11.9~bp [7.4, 16.4] with firm fixed effects; indistinguishable from zero on CSI~300 alone, $-0.6$~bp [$-15.8$, $+14.6$]).

Attempting to match the 28.0~bp association revealed a sharp saturation boundary (Figure~\ref{fig:regimes}b and Table~\ref{tab:regimes}). Simply scaling the contract spread made baseline debt rates unstable (37 of 42 development runs exhausted firm value). Adding a direct cross-sectionally centred rating channel $r_i = r_f + s E_i/(E_i+P_i) - \lambda(z_i - \bar z)$ stabilised all 1,590 confirmatory runs on fresh seeds (57001--57030) under form~$Q$ across $\lambda \in \{0, 9.68, 21.2, 25.75, 30.3\}$~bp. Every single one of the eight Holm-corrected primary contrasts at $\lambda^* = 25.75$~bp---and all 40 sensitivity intervals across the five $\lambda$ values---kept its predicted sign and excluded zero. Yet between $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{FE}} = 9.68$~bp, the system undergoes a \emph{saturation transition}: baseline inflating leaps from 21.4\% to 85.8\% (and 93.5\% at $\lambda^*$, with 92.7--95.0\% across every size tier). Two structural consequences follow: (i)~most firms bunch near the rating ceiling (interquartile signal width collapses from 1.43 to 0.11), destroying empirical rank persistence (lag-12 autocorrelation plunges from 0.55 to 0.05; re-estimating dynamics at $\lambda^*$ yields best $T = 497$); and (ii)~cross-tier displacement shares compress sharply (small-firm audit shift falls from $+22.4$ to $+0.34$~pp and laxest-assurer shift from $+70.1$ to $+16.4$~pp) because near-universal inflation leaves almost no margin between size tiers, even as the true-ESG penalty of firm-chosen assurance widens tenfold from $-0.013$ to $-0.139$~SD. Our primary displacement magnitudes therefore hold strictly in the unsaturated regime where financing incentives remain consistent with observed rating persistence.

\subsection{Empirical scope and remaining structural limitations}\label{sec:validity}

\paragraph{Investor response.}
Our baseline contract relies on investors pricing reported ESG. A pre-registered empirical test on the twelve as-released quarterly Huazheng updates from January 2023 to October 2025 (Investor-Response Study; Appendix~\ref{app:investors}) supports two of three primary questions after Holm correction: a one-notch annual upgrade accompanies $+0.12$~pp [$+0.07$, $+0.17$] higher year-end fund ownership of tradable shares, and a one-SD higher score accompanies an implied cost of equity 14.5~bp lower [$+3.0$, $+26.0$], whereas two-month post-update returns show no significant response ($+0.14$~pp [$-0.17$, $+0.45$]). These are associations (and the fund-ownership result weakens when dropping the 2024 methodology break or using normal-score changes). Under policy in the Persistence Study, simulated investors react more strongly than the data (2.33~pp quarterly price move per SD; 32.4~bp policy-window yield slope vs.\ 25.6~bp pre-policy), although model prices react without release delays. Because taste moves yield slopes, inflating rates and rating distributions jointly, we did not recalibrate taste downward after the empirical investor check; fund-level ESG holdings remain untested.

\paragraph{Policy costs and institutional coverage.}
Neither the administrative cost of raising transparency nor the supervisory cost of running audits is modelled, so our fixed-dose rankings do not constitute social-welfare or equal-cost comparisons. There are no endogenous credit or ESG rating agencies, no reputational exit or mandatory rotation for assurance providers, and---as bounded in Section~\ref{sec:targeted}---no Stackelberg-optimal dynamic audit solver.

\paragraph{Firm rationality, data and market-layer qualification.}
All main institutional comparisons assume myopic NPV-maximising firms; as Section~\ref{sec:learning} documents, unmodified account-level tabular Q-learning fails coverage due to an extreme signal-to-noise barrier, leaving variance-reduced bandits and socially imitative firms for future work. Empirically, CSI~300 covers large constituents at a single composition snapshot, and ratings remain proxies whose publication dates can lag rating dates. Finally, in a pre-policy qualification audit of 60 fixed market worlds, 57 passed and 3 failed mechanical checks; 37 of 1,770 Study~1 runs and 29 of 540 Study~3 runs triggered the daily log-return clamp (with zero divergence aborts); and the frozen market-microstructure fit leaves lag-1 return autocorrelation too negative ($-0.18$ vs.\ $-0.03$). Every failed and guarded run remains in the public archive, and excluding guarded runs changes no primary verdict.

%======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%======================================================================

Every enforcement instrument we tested leaves an institutional gap, and strategic agents route greenwashing straight into it. Fixed per-case fines concentrate inflation among large firms and open undetected cases; public heuristic audit rules push inflation onto lightly audited small firms or below jump-detection thresholds while raising audit hit rates; and client-chosen assurance providers trigger annual escape cycles that funnel 96.9\% of assured greenwashing to the year's laxest provider and nullify most of mandatory assurance's gain. Even under random provider assignment, coupled firm--assurer dynamics generate endogenous quality cycles and non-monotonic liability returns where $100\times$ liability performs worse than $16\times$. Disclosure transparency leaves far less room for arbitrage because it discounts every inflated report uniformly across firm size, case history, audit schedules and assurance providers, outperforming quadrupled fixed fines across the 20 specifications and replications in Table~\ref{tab:robust}.

Viewed as a mechanism and institutional design study for multi-agent systems, our results offer three lessons beyond single-agent inspection theory. First, normative enforcement mechanisms should be evaluated by \emph{where they displace noncompliance} and by their cross-agent feedback loops, not by local detection hit rates or static single-agent snapshots: both targeted audit rules caught inflation far more often than random audits yet lowered aggregate compliance once public, and counterfactual replay shows that near-zero net market feedback under transparency masks opposing evolutionary ($-12\%$) and price--demand ($+9.8\%$) channels. Second, institutional effect sizes sit behind sharp structural phase boundaries: compliance-arbitrage displacement shares remain large across uniform decay, no decay and permanent-plus-transitory dynamics $Q$, whereas true-ESG magnitudes collapse by one to two orders of magnitude whenever deliberate improvement fades faster than $\tau = 0.6$ can clear $\tau^*$ or whenever direct rating incentives saturate reporting. Third, strict pre-registration, paired counterfactual replays and full archival of failed runs---including rejected dynamic fits, rating-ceiling saturation and the account-level MARL signal-to-noise barrier---provide a falsifiable experimental template for multi-agent policy design.

Four concrete multi-agent research directions follow directly from the boundaries mapped here: computing Stackelberg-optimal dynamic audit schedules that account for open-case persistence and size heterogeneity \citep{tambe2011security,blocki2015audit}; testing mandatory rotation, reputation dynamics, runtime sanction revision \citep{dellanna2020runtime} and noisy ex-post observability in assurance markets; modelling boundedly rational firms via variance-reduced bandits, difference rewards \citep{agogino2008analyzing} or inter-firm social imitation \citep{schlag1998imitate}; and obtaining verified multi-year rating and event-level disclosure panels to identify whether deliberate ESG improvements persist or revert.

\subsection{Mean reversion (exploratory)}\label{app:reversion}

The evidence on decay does not support uniform decay. In a quarterly panel of Huazheng ESG ratings and composite scores for all A-share firms from January 2009 to October 2025 (225,754 firm-dates, 5,816 firms, 68 quarterly dates; obtained from a secondary source, internally consistent with the annual October file, and spot-checked on its October 2025 wave against a non-random official-website snapshot of 119 CSI~300 firms, though historical waves remain unverified against an official Wind or CSMAR export), two clear methodology breaks split the series in April 2021 (adjacent same-grade rate drops from 87.9\% to 33.6\% and score SD halves from 6.64 to 3.73) and April 2024 (same-grade rate drops from 88.3\% to 26.8\%, score SD doubles from 4.24 to 8.67, and AAA grades first appear). Within the longest single-methodology regime, R1 (January 2009--January 2021, 49 dates, 129,382 firm-dates), the average rating has no trend (yearly means between 3.94 in 2010 and 4.19 in 2012 on the 1--9 scale, overall mean 4.08), and ratings revert towards the middle: across grades with more than 100 observations, the mean next-quarter change is $+0.25$ notches for C (the lowest grade) and $-0.27$ notches for A (the highest; specifically C $+0.25$, CC $+0.20$, CCC $+0.10$, B $0.00$, BB $-0.10$, BBB $-0.18$, A $-0.27$). Mean reversion is a different functional form from uniform decay and is not covered by the range above. The panel may not settle the level either: if ratings are relative to peers, an economy-wide trend cannot be seen in them.

We therefore added a mean-reverting form: the true level reverts towards the day's cross-sectional mean at rate $\kappa$ per year, with idiosyncratic shocks that keep the cross-sectional variance at one, and NPV firms account for the reversion when they value improvement. In R1, the pooled one-quarter autocorrelation is 0.896 for discrete grades (20.9\% quarterly grade-change rate) and 0.929 for composite scores, implying $\kappa = -4\ln\rho_1 = 0.441$ (grade) and $0.294$ (score), or after removing white-noise measurement error via $\kappa = -(4/3)\ln(\rho_4/\rho_1)$, $0.352$ (grade) and $0.321$ (score); the shorter later regimes R2 and R3 imply slower reversion (0.19--0.43). The exploratory supplement (contract Section~20.21) and joint robustness test (Appendix~\ref{app:joint}) were run at $\kappa = 0.32$ and $\kappa = 0.50$, values computed from an earlier 2009--2022 copy of the panel before the 2009--2025 panel was obtained. Column-by-column audit later showed that the 2009--2022 file shifted date labels two months late and mixed six 2021--2022 columns across the April 2021 methodology break (which had depressed its apparent lag-1 autocorrelation to 0.88 and overstated the top-grade next-quarter drop as $-0.48$). Because the tested pair $\kappa \in \{0.32, 0.50\}$ still brackets R1's grade-based values (0.35 and 0.44) and sits just above its score-based lag-1 value (0.29), those runs remain informative as sensitivity checks, while Appendix~\ref{app:persistence} estimates natural dynamics formally on the clean R1 score autocorrelations. We treat $\kappa = 0.32$ and $0.50$ as data-motivated sensitivity values, not as estimates or bounds of how fast the true level reverts: the panel's historical waves are unverified against an official export, and its reversion mixes measurement noise, rating discreteness and firms' own actions, whose net effect on these numbers we cannot sign. Under both rates the ordering I $>$ F $>$ B survives, but the advantage of information almost disappears. The gap between the instruments is $+0.006$~SD [$+0.004$, $+0.008$] at $\kappa = 0.32$ and $+0.003$ [$+0.002$, $+0.004$] at $\kappa = 0.50$, and the effect of information against the baseline is $+0.027$ and $+0.021$. Both lie below the lower end of the uniform-decay range by amounts the paired intervals resolve. The fixed-fine effect, $+0.021$ and $+0.018$, stays inside it. The mechanism is the one Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} predicts. Reversion erodes real improvement (half-lives of 1.4--2.2 years) while inflation is removed only by audits, so $A_r$ falls from 3.73 to 2.01 and 1.51 years, and the threshold at $w = 0.2$ rises to 0.77 and 0.82. Transparency of 0.6 no longer stops inflation: 20--22\% of decisions still inflate under I, against 25--28\% under B, so transparency acts much like a heavier fine. Reversion is thus not a decay rate between the two ends of the range. Like no decay, it does not pull the average down; like strong decay, it makes room for inflation; and it lowers the value of real improvement.

\subsection{A fine for every false report (exploratory)}\label{app:perreport}

When every false report is fined separately from day~0, and NPV firms charge each new inflation its expected fine, the share of inflating decisions falls from 14.3\% at the statutory fine to 6.6\%, 2.4\% and 0.8\% at 2, 4 and 8 times it. Under per-case booking the corresponding shares are 22.3\%, 15.5\%, 13.3\% and 12.5\%. Deterrence still diminishes: each doubling adds $+0.094$, $+0.064$ and $+0.034$~SD, and each step is smaller than the last by an amount the paired intervals resolve. It diminishes because each doubling deters fewer and larger firms, for which the fine is a smaller share of value; the remaining inflation comes from the largest firms. The fine becomes a stronger instrument ($+0.158$~SD against the baseline, from $+0.125$), the baseline itself improves, and information is unaffected because it stops inflation outright. The gap between information and the quadrupled fine therefore shrinks from $+0.205$ to $+0.058$~SD [$+0.042$, $+0.074$], still positive. Which booking is right is a legal question: several years of false disclosure are often handled in a single penalty decision, but a separate fine per false report is also a plausible reading of the law.

\subsection{Firm-specific washing costs (exploratory)}\label{app:hetero}

We drew each firm's ratio $w_i$ from a lognormal distribution with mean 0.2 and log-SD $\ln 2$ (5th to 95th percentile 0.05 to 0.49). Inflation then no longer stops at a common threshold. At $\tau = 0.6$, 7.3\% of decisions still inflate (none with a common ratio), in every seed. At $\tau = 0.8$, 0.4\% do, in 29 of 30 seeds. The decline is governed firm by firm: all 53,015 inflating decisions after the switch, across the three transparency levels, came from firms whose own threshold $\tau^*(w_i)$ exceeded the current $\tau$, as Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} implies. The policy comparison barely moves. Information beats the baseline by $+0.320$~SD (common ratio $+0.331$) and the fine by $+0.202$ ($+0.205$). The plateau above $\tau = 0.6$ in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} partly reflects the common ratio: with dispersed ratios, raising transparency from 0.6 to 0.8 still cuts inflation from 7.3\% to 0.4\%.

\subsection{Both assumptions together (pre-registered)}\label{app:joint}

Both assumptions narrow the gap, mean reversion by weakening transparency and per-report fines by strengthening the fixed fine, so together they could have let the quadrupled fine catch up with transparency. It did not, because a fine for every report also applies under I, which keeps the baseline fine: inflation under I falls from 18--21\% of decisions with reversion alone to 0.8--1.3\%, and under F to 2.2--2.4\%. Proposition~\ref{prop:threshold} implies the same ordering of who inflates: with a fine per report, a firm that inflates under I in a given state would also inflate under F, as long as its choice is not at the per-period cap. The advantage of transparency is $+0.012$~SD [$+0.009$, $+0.015$] at $\kappa = 0.32$ and $+0.006$ [$+0.005$, $+0.008$] at $\kappa = 0.50$; firm-specific washing costs lower it by about 0.002. The reference blocks of the same run reproduce the earlier exploratory values on the new seeds (main setting $+0.200$, fine per report $+0.067$, reversion alone $+0.006$ and $+0.002$).

\paragraph{What remains untested.} The pure-reversion rates above are data-motivated sensitivity values from a secondary-source panel (partially checked on its latest wave), not estimates or bounds; rates below 0.32 (including R1's score-based lag-1 value of 0.29 and R2/R3 rates of 0.19--0.43), reversion combined with decay, and firms that ignore reversion were not run in the pure-reversion supplement. The per-report booking was tested only in the main setting and together with mean reversion. Appendix~\ref{app:persistence} formally estimates persistence on R1 scores and re-runs the main, targeted-audit and assurance comparisons under the closest fitted form.

\subsection{Rating persistence estimation and confirmatory re-run (stage~H, pre-registered)}\label{app:persistence}

\paragraph{Data targets and candidate dynamics (stage~H2).} Before any implementation or simulation, we fixed the estimation targets, candidate natural dynamics, selection rule and confirmatory re-run in the research contract (\S25; committed as \texttt{eb9efdd}). Using the longest single-methodology regime of the Huazheng panel, R1 (January 2009--January 2021, 49 quarterly dates, 4,065 A-share firms; Appendix~\ref{app:reversion}), we convert each date's composite score to a rank-normal score $\Phi^{-1}((\text{rank}-0.5)/n)$ and compute pooled firm-level autocorrelations at lags $k \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 12\}$ quarters: $0.9288$, $0.8635$, $0.7410$, $0.6287$ and $0.5475$ (firm-clustered bootstrap standard errors $0.0012$, $0.0024$, $0.0047$, $0.0069$ and $0.0088$, 1,000 draws). Pure geometric decay from lag~1 ($0.929^{12} \approx 0.41$) lies well below the observed lag-12 correlation ($0.548$), indicating a persistent firm-specific component.

We compare three natural-dynamics forms in baseline markets (B) with continuous NPV firms:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Uniform decay} $U(\delta)$: the main stage~F form ($\delta \in [0, 0.30]$~SD/year; 6 grid points).
\item \emph{Mean reversion} $R(\kappa)$: the form of Appendix~\ref{app:reversion} ($\kappa \in [0.1, 1.0]$ per year; 8 grid points).
\item \emph{Permanent-plus-transitory} $Q(\kappa_T, s_P)$: $g_i = \mu_i + x_i$, where $\mu_i$ is a time-invariant firm anchor with cross-sectional variance $s_P$ and $x_i$ reverts towards its cross-sectional mean at rate $\kappa_T$ per year with idiosyncratic shocks preserving variance $1 - s_P$, initialised so that the initial distribution of $g_{i0}$ is unchanged ($\kappa_T \in [0.25, 3.0] \times s_P \in [0.1, 0.7]$; 49 grid points). In the main specification of $Q$, deliberate improvement enters $x_i$ and fades at rate $\kappa_T$; an accompanying permanent-improvement block (\texttt{perm\_}) adds deliberate improvement to $\mu_i$ so that improvement lasts while shocks still revert.
\end{itemize}
All three forms share an auxiliary post-processing rating measurement error $\sigma_m \in [0, 0.6]$ added before rank-normalisation and unobserved by investors. Each of the 63 grid points is run on 10 estimation seeds (48001--48010; 630 runs, all completed), sampling public signals every 63 trading days from day 1260 (36 quarterly dates). Using cubic/bicubic spline interpolation across the grid, $\hat\theta$ minimises the simulated minimum-distance criterion $J(\theta) = g(\theta)' W g(\theta)$, where $g(\theta) = m_{\text{data}} - \bar m_{\text{sim}}(\theta)$ and $W = \operatorname{diag}(\hat\Omega_{\text{data}})^{-1}$. Model fit is tested by $T = g(\hat\theta)' W g(\hat\theta)$, whose asymptotic null distribution is a weighted $\chi^2$ evaluated by $10^6$ Monte Carlo draws.

\paragraph{Estimation, rejection and recovery failure.} All three forms are rejected by the data ($p < 10^{-6}$). Uniform decay hits the upper grid bound ($\hat\delta = 0.30$, $\hat\sigma_m = 0.225$, grid $T = 2{,}573$): because uniform decay does not reshuffle firm ranks, its lag-12 correlation stays at $0.774$ against $0.548$ in the data. Pure mean reversion ($R$: $\hat\kappa = 0.233$ [0.209, 0.257], $\hat\sigma_m = 0.108$, grid $T = 130$) overshoots lag~4 ($0.776$ vs.\ $0.741$) and undershoots lag~12 ($0.486$ vs.\ $0.548$). Form~$Q$ comes closest ($\hat\kappa_T = 0.505$, $\hat s_P = 0.423$, $\hat\sigma_m = 0$ at the lower bound, grid $T = 37.8$). Re-evaluated on 30 seeds (48001--48030) at $(\kappa_T, s_P) = (0.505, 0.423)$, form~$Q$ yields $T = 38.7$ ($p < 10^{-6}$), with model correlations $0.9260$, $0.8660$, $0.7672$, $0.6303$ and $0.5397$: lags 2, 8 and 12 lie inside their 95\% bands, lag~1 is $0.0029$ below the data (half-width $0.0027$) and lag~4 is $0.0262$ above (half-width $0.0100$), likely reflecting Huazheng's concentrated April annual-report reassessment, which is not in the model. Sandwich 95\% intervals are $\hat\kappa_T \in [0.467, 0.542]$ and $\hat s_P \in [0.374, 0.473]$, reflecting sampling error only and not misspecification. On the 271 current CSI~300 constituents observed in R1 (robustness only), $Q$ gives similar estimates ($\hat\kappa_T = 0.465$, $\hat s_P = 0.339$, $T = 3.3$, all five lags inside bands, overall $p < 10^{-6}$), while $U$ remains strongly rejected ($T = 222$).

Following the pre-registered selection rule (contract \S25.6), because no candidate fits, $Q$ is selected as the smallest-$T$ form and flagged as rejected by the data. In the pre-registered parameter-recovery check (three pseudo-data groups of 10 markets each, seeds 52001--52030), all three groups select $Q$, but $\hat\kappa_T$ is biased upward by $+0.02$ to $+0.045$ (4--9\%, two of three groups outside the main sandwich interval) while $\hat s_P$ lies inside in all three; recovery is therefore recorded as failed, indicating weak identification. A subsequent code audit (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}) identified that the archived H2 script computed auxiliary full-covariance profile intervals relative to $J^*(\hat\theta)$ at the diagonal-weight point estimate rather than $\min J^*$; because the primary point estimates, fit statistics, recovery checks and policy contrasts do not depend on those auxiliary profiles, we omit the affected archived ranges.

\paragraph{Confirmatory re-run (stage~H3).} Extending targeted audits and third-party assurance to mean-reverting dynamics (committed as \texttt{905138a}; frozen configuration committed as \texttt{e24c7ca}), we re-ran the core, targeted-audit and assurance comparisons at $Q(\kappa_T = 0.505, s_P = 0.423)$, together with a permanent-improvement block (\texttt{perm\_}) and a uniform-decay reference block (\texttt{u0p2\_}): 24 scenarios $\times$ 30 fresh paired seeds (53001--53030) = 720 runs, all completed with zero failures and zero price-guard hits.

\subsection{An in-sample financing premium (pre-registered)}\label{app:financing}

\paragraph{Estimate.} Before estimating, we fixed the sample, variables and specification in the research contract (\S26). In a panel of non-financial A-share firms, we regress the debt cost in year $t+1$ (interest expense over average interest-bearing debt) on the year-$t$ Huazheng composite score, converted to a normal score within each rating date, with controls for size, leverage, profitability and state ownership, industry-by-year fixed effects and standard errors clustered by firm. The estimate was run once. On 41,662 firm-years of 4,706 firms (2009--2024), a one-SD higher score goes with a debt cost 28.0 basis points lower [23.4, 32.5]. In fourteen of fifteen robustness specifications the estimate is positive and its interval excludes zero; the thirteen of them measured per SD range from 11.9 to 36.2 basis points, and with firm fixed effects the estimate is 11.9 [7.4, 16.4]. The exception is the subsample of current CSI~300 constituents, $-0.6$ [$-15.8$, $+14.6$], indistinguishable from zero. Bond issue spreads point the same way, 6.8 [2.4, 11.3]. These are associations, not causal effects; if part of the association reflects firm quality correlated with the rating, the model overstates firms' incentive. By coincidence the estimate is close to the cost-of-equity association of about 28 basis points per SD that the investor taste targets \citep{zhao2024esg}; the two are different quantities.

\paragraph{Mapping into the model.} The model's counterpart is $\rho$, the local response of a firm's financing rate to its own signal, about 2.2 basis points per SD. A first pre-registered rule raised only the contract spread, to 0.606 from 0.05. This also raised the level of the contract rate, to 22.7\% at the start, and 37 of 42 development runs at that spread failed, 36 of them because financing charges exhausted firm value. No confirmatory run was started. The amended rule keeps the spread and lets the debt rate respond directly to the rating:
\[
r_i = r_f + s\,\frac{E_i}{E_i+P_i} - \lambda\,(z_i - \bar z),
\]
where $z_i$ is the latest public signal and $\bar z$ its equal-weighted cross-sectional mean on the quote date. With $\lambda^* = 25.75$ basis points per unit of the signal, the median pre-policy response is 28.1 basis points. The model counts one unit of the signal as one SD, while the cross-sectional SD of signals is about 0.67, so per cross-sectional SD the channel is about 17 basis points. The channel prices the rating relative to other firms: the equal-weighted mean of the direct term is zero on every quote date, but the exposure-weighted mean is not.

\paragraph{Confirmatory re-run.} We re-ran the main, targeted-audit and assurance scenarios at $\lambda^*$ under the permanent-plus-transitory dynamics $Q(\kappa_T = 0.505, s_P = 0.423)$ selected in Appendix~\ref{app:persistence} (stage~H), together with a sensitivity block at four other values of $\lambda$ including $\lambda = 0$: 1,590 runs on new seeds (57001--57030), all completed. All eight Holm-corrected primary questions keep their predicted sign and are distinguishable from zero (Table~\ref{tab:financing}). The contrasts in where greenwashing goes shrink sharply. At $\lambda^*$, 93.5\% of baseline decisions are inflating ones, against 21.4\% at $\lambda = 0$; within each size class the rate is 93--95\%, so each class's share of inflating decisions is close to its fixed share of all decisions, and no instrument has much room to shift it. Contrasts in the true level partly grow and partly shrink. Across the five values of $\lambda$, all 40 uncorrected intervals exclude zero with the predicted sign; most of the change in magnitude occurs between $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda = 9.7$ basis points.

\paragraph{Rating persistence under the channel.} The natural dynamics $Q$ used in the re-run were estimated at $\lambda = 0$ (Appendix~\ref{app:persistence}). Replaying the baseline at $\lambda^*$ shows that the rank autocorrelation of ratings falls from 0.93 to 0.67 at a lag of one quarter and from 0.55 to 0.05 at twelve quarters, against 0.93 and 0.55 in the data, far outside the data's sampling error. A pre-registered re-estimation of the natural dynamics at $\lambda^*$ (stage~I v3, using the estimator of Appendix~\ref{app:persistence}) found that none of the three forms fits; the best, uniform decay of 0.15~SD per year, has a fit statistic of $T = 497$, against $T = 38.7$ for form~$Q$ selected at $\lambda = 0$ (Appendix~\ref{app:persistence}), and a pre-set rule stopped the confirmatory re-run at the new estimate. (Stage~I v3's archived auxiliary profile intervals inherit the same reference-minimum bug corrected in contract \S28 and are not used.) The reason is that at $\lambda^*$ most firms push their reported signals close to the top of the scale. In the last quarter under the re-estimated dynamics, the middle half of firms lies within a band 0.11 wide, against 1.43 at $\lambda = 0$, so ratings no longer separate firms and their ranks are fragile to small measurement errors.

\paragraph{Reading.} In this model, a direct rating response of the estimated size makes most firms bunch at the top of the scale, which is incompatible with the persistence of Huazheng ratings. This is a statement about the model, with its cap on actions per period, its ceiling on reports and its reduced-form contract; it does not say that real firms have no financing premium. We therefore keep the smaller financing incentive for the main results and report the re-run beside them: the signs of the eight contrasts do not depend on $\lambda$, but their magnitudes do, and should not be extrapolated to a financing incentive that matches the sample. Under the direct channel, firms' financing incentive no longer depends on investors' taste, so the zero-taste control of the main experiments applies to the original contract only.

\subsection{Do investors respond to Huazheng ratings? (pre-registered)}\label{app:investors}

\paragraph{Design.} Before estimating, we fixed the samples, variables, specifications and inference in the research contract (\S27); the three primary questions are Holm-corrected, and the estimate was run once. Huazheng rates firms on 31 January, 30 April, 31 July and 31 October and generally publishes each update within twenty trading days. Ratings before 2018 were back-calculated when the product launched, and the ratings of 2018-10 to 2022-07 in our panel differ from those released at the time, so the confirmatory sample is the twelve updates from January 2023 to October 2025, whose ratings we infer to be as released. J1 regresses the compounded return of the two months after the rating-date month on the change in the rating in notches (C${}=1$ to AAA${}=9$), with size, book-to-market, the return of the rating quarter, momentum and the previous rating as controls and industry effects within each date, and averages the twelve cross-sectional slopes (Fama--MacBeth, $t$ with 11 degrees of freedom). J2 regresses the change in year-end fund ownership, as a percentage of tradable A shares, on the change in the rating from October to October, with industry-by-year effects and errors clustered by firm. J4 regresses the implied cost of equity at the end of June $t+1$ on the October-$t$ score, converted to a normal score within the date: $r = \sqrt{(\mathrm{EPS}_2 - \mathrm{EPS}_1)/P_0}$ \citep{easton2004pe}, with the median of each broker's latest forecast in the previous 180 days, at least two brokers, each forecast restated to the June share count, and the controls of Appendix~\ref{app:financing} plus book-to-market. A planned test on the holdings of ESG-themed funds (J3) was not done, because fund-level holdings were not available to us.

\paragraph{Results.} Two of the three questions are supported (Table~\ref{tab:investors}). A one-notch upgrade over the year goes with year-end fund ownership 0.12 percentage points of tradable shares higher, against a mean ownership of 5.1\% and a standard deviation of the yearly change of 5.3 points. A one-SD higher score goes with an implied cost of equity 14.5 basis points lower, against a median cost of 10.8\%. Returns after an update show no statistically significant response; of the robustness checks, only the cross-section of April 2024, the date of Huazheng's method revision, is distinguishable from zero ($+0.59$ [$+0.27$, $+0.91$]). On the back-calculated ratings, neither fund ownership nor the cost of equity responds distinguishably from zero, but the differences between the two periods are not statistically significant either ($+0.11$ [$-0.01$, $+0.22$] and $+11.5$ [$-2.4$, $+25.4$]), and the periods differ in method and market as well. The cost-of-equity estimate is stable across the pre-registered variants (14.5 to 16.2 basis points, each interval excluding zero; with at least three brokers the interval, [4.1, 28.3], contains 28). The fund-ownership estimate is not: with the change in the normal score as treatment it is $+0.07$ [$-0.02$, $+0.16$] per SD, and without 2024 $+0.07$ [$-0.01$, $+0.14$]. J4 covers only firms with at least two brokers' forecasts, 44\% of the eligible firm-years, and these firms are rated higher than the rest (mean score 0.39 SD).

\paragraph{Comparison with the model.} The model's counterparts come from replaying the baseline of the stage~H3 re-run under form~$Q$ on its 30 seeds (Appendix~\ref{app:persistence}). A one-SD change in the public signal moves $\log(P/V)$ by 2.33 percentage points within a quarter [2.13, 2.54], against $+0.12$ [$-0.52$, $+0.77$] per SD of the change in the normal score in the data. The slope of dividend yield on the signal is 25.6 basis points per cross-sectional SD before any policy and 32.4 over the policy window, against the cost-of-equity estimate of 14.5 [3.0, 26.0]. The pre-policy slope lies inside the interval; the price response and the policy-window slope do not. The comparison is not like for like: the model updates the signal daily and prices respond at once, whereas the data measure the response within the release window, and the model's dividend yield is not an analyst-based cost of equity.

\paragraph{Reading.} Investors in our sample do respond to Huazheng ratings through fund ownership and the cost of equity, which supports the direction of the model's central assumption; the response is smaller than the model's under policy, and the return response is not distinguishable from zero. We did not change the investor taste, for the reasons given in Section~\ref{sec:validity}.

\paragraph{Noise-trader demand and investor cash-cap scaling.}
Each day, the noise-trader account updates AR(1) common and firm-specific innovations $z_t^{\mathrm{com}} = 0.5 z_{t-1}^{\mathrm{com}} + \sqrt{0.75}\,\varepsilon_t^{\mathrm{com}}$ and $z_{i,t}^{\mathrm{id}} = 0.5 z_{i,t-1}^{\mathrm{id}} + \sqrt{0.75}\,\varepsilon_{i,t}^{\mathrm{id}}$ with standard normal draws, scales demand by capped relative volatility $d_{i,t} = \big(\sqrt{0.80}\,z_t^{\mathrm{com}} + \sqrt{0.20}\,z_{i,t}^{\mathrm{id}}\big)\min\!\big(\sqrt{v_{i,t}/v_{i,t}^{\mathrm{ref}}},\,4\big)^{1.77}$, and submits raw order
\[
q_{i,t}^{\mathrm{noise}} = 0.002\,d_{i,t} - 0.05\,\big(h_{i,t-1}^{\mathrm{noise}} - 0.05\big),
\]
floored at $-h_{i,t-1}^{\mathrm{noise}}$ (no short sales) and subject to the same cash-cap rule applied to every investor account: given cash $C_j$, buys $b_{ji} = \max(q_{ji}, 0)$ and sells $s_{ji} = \min(q_{ji}, 0)$, available cash after sales is $A_j = C_j - (1 - c_{\mathrm{tx}})\sum_i s_{ji} P_i$; if gross purchases $B_j^{\mathrm{buy}} = (1 + c_{\mathrm{tx}})\sum_i b_{ji} P_i > A_j$, all buys scale uniformly by $\max(A_j, 0)/B_j^{\mathrm{buy}}$.

\paragraph{Local permanent log-price response $\partial \ln P_i / \partial s_i$.}
On each firm decision day, the firm layer evaluates the local permanent price response to firm~$i$'s public signal $s_i$ by one-sided finite difference ($\epsilon = 10^{-4}$) over all $M = 1{,}000$ investors' single-step rebalances. Let $h^*_{ji}$ be investor~$j$'s baseline softmax-tilted target share holding, $w_{ji} = h^*_{ji} P_i / \sum_\ell h^*_{j\ell} P_\ell$ its risky equity weight, and $q_{ji}^{\mathrm{base}}$ its resulting short-sale-floored, cash-capped rebalance order. Perturbing firm~$i$'s signal by $+\epsilon$ shifts investor~$j$'s relative tilt by $\rho_j = e^{\epsilon \kappa_j \theta_j} - 1$, yielding perturbed target shares $h^\epsilon_{j\ell} = h^*_{j\ell} / (1 + w_{ji}\rho_j)$ for $\ell \neq i$ and $h^\epsilon_{ji} = h^*_{ji}(1 + \rho_j)/(1 + w_{ji}\rho_j)$ and cash-capped perturbed order $q^\epsilon_{ji}$. The permanent log-price response is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \ln P_i}{\partial s_i} = \max\!\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\sum_{j=1}^M \big(q^\epsilon_{ji} - q_{ji}^{\mathrm{base}}\big),\; 0\right)\frac{\lambda_p\,\eta_{\mathrm{perm}}}{\phi_0},
\label{eq:dlogpds}
\end{equation}
which enters $B_i$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:benefit}. When the direct rating-linked debt channel ($\lambda > 0$, Appendix~\ref{app:financing}) is active, firms take the cross-sectional mean $\bar z$ as given, so $B_i = \frac{E_i}{P_i}\!\left[\frac{s E_i P_i}{(E_i+P_i)^2}\frac{\partial \ln P_i}{\partial s_i} + \lambda\right]$.

\paragraph{Extended annuity and fine-factor formulas.}
The baseline factors $A_r, A_w, D$ of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:annuities}--\eqref{eq:finefactor} extend in closed form across the paper's structural variants:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Transitory mean reversion ($R$ and $Q$).} When deliberate real improvement reverts at annual rate $\kappa$, the fraction still present $d = q(u) - t_r \ge 0$ days after implementation is $\psi(d) = \frac{1}{N} + \big(1 - \frac{1}{N}\big)e^{-\kappa d/252}$ (accounting for firm~$i$'s $1/N$ weight in the cross-sectional mean), giving $A_r = \frac{1}{252}\sum_{u=1}^H \mathbf{1}_{\{q(u)\ge t_r\}}\,\psi\!\big(q(u)-t_r\big)\,e^{-r_d u/252}$. Under uniform decay, no decay, or the permanent-improvement $Q$ block, $\psi(d) \equiv 1$.
\item \emph{Displaced-inflation real improvement.} When real improvement exceeds remaining report room $C - g_i - x_i$ (net of pending actions), each unit beyond the room displaces one unit of existing undetected inflation $x_i$, earning marginal annuity
\[
A_r^{\mathrm{disp}} = A_r - (1 - \tau)\,\frac{1}{252}\sum_{u=1}^H \mathbf{1}_{\{q(u)\ge t_r\}}\,(1 - q_i)^{n_a^{\mathrm{old}}(u)}(1 - a_i)^{n_c(u)}\,e^{-r_d u/252},
\]
where $n_a^{\mathrm{old}}(u) = \max(0, \lfloor (q(u)-1)/P_d \rfloor - \lfloor t_0/P_d \rfloor)$ counts audits on old inflation; the firm optimises the resulting piecewise-quadratic NPV across the kink at remaining report room.
\item \emph{Public jump-targeted audits (Study~2).} Under public rule~J, the first audit after disclosure has probability $q_{i,1}$ (either $0.5\bar q$ if unflagged or $\min(1, 0.5\bar q + 0.5K/\max(1, m_t))$ if flagged) while later audits use $q_i = 0.5\bar q$. For $n_a(u) \ge 1$, wash survival becomes $(1 - q_{i,1})(1 - q_i)^{n_a(u)-1}$, and the detection discount becomes
\[
D = e^{-r_d(t_{a,1}-t_0)/252}\left[q_{i,1} + \frac{(1 - q_{i,1})\,q_i\,e^{-r_d P_d/252}}{1 - (1 - q_i)e^{-r_d P_d/252}}\right].
\]
Under transitory reversion ($Q$ in the Persistence Study), the sub-threshold headroom adds the expected single-period drift of firm~$i$'s transitory component: $x_i^{\mathrm{safe}} = h - \Delta_i^{\mathrm{pending}} + \delta P_d/252 + \big(1 - e^{-\kappa_T P_d/252}\big)(x_i^{\mathrm{trans}} - \bar x^{\mathrm{trans}})$.
\item \emph{Pre-publication assurance checks (Study~3).} With per-period provider check probability $a_i$ on every disclosure day $t_w + k P_d$, let $n_c(u) = \mathbf{1}_{\{q(u)\ge t_w\}}(\lfloor (q(u)-t_w)/P_d \rfloor + 1)$ count checks up to quote day $q(u)$ and $c_0 = \lfloor (t_{a,1} - 1 - t_w)/P_d \rfloor + 1$ count checks strictly before the first regulatory audit. Then
\begin{align*}
A_w(a_i) &= \frac{1}{252}\sum_{u=1}^H \mathbf{1}_{\{q(u)\ge t_w\}}\,(1 - q_i)^{n_a(u)}(1 - a_i)^{n_c(u)}\,e^{-r_d u/252},\\
D(a_i) &= \frac{q_i\,(1 - a_i)^{c_0}\,e^{-r_d(t_{a,1}-t_0)/252}}{1 - (1 - q_i)(1 - a_i)e^{-r_d P_d/252}}.
\end{align*}
\end{enumerate}
